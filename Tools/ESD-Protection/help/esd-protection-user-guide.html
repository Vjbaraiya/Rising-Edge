1. Introduction

Purpose

The ESD Protection Analyzer is a browser-based engineering tool that automates the identification of ESD-vulnerable signals in PCB schematics and recommends appropriate TVS protection components. It is part of the Rising Edge Technologies engineering tool suite, which is designed to accelerate hardware design review and reduce costly PCB spins caused by unprotected ESD entry points.

Intended Users

The tool is designed for hardware engineers, PCB designers, signal integrity engineers, and EMC compliance engineers who work on products with external connectors, user-accessible interfaces, or connectivity to cables and the outside world. The tool assumes a working knowledge of schematic design but includes explanatory content suitable for engineers who are new to ESD protection design.

What the Tool Does

The tool extracts text from a schematic PDF, identifies signal net names matching known ESD-vulnerable interface patterns (USB, Ethernet, CAN, UART, audio, RF, and more), maps each signal to its recommended TVS device, and generates a structured protection requirements table along with PCB layout guidance. It also provides interactive charts showing the ESD clamping waveform and a capacitance-vs-data-rate budget chart for all recommended devices.

What the Tool Does Not Do

The tool works by pattern-matching net names against a known signal database. It cannot interpret schematic topology from the PDF, cannot detect signals with non-standard or custom net names, and cannot perform full-wave electromagnetic simulation. The results should be used as a systematic starting point for ESD design review, not as a substitute for component-level characterization or pre-compliance EMC testing.

Supported Standards

The tool's signal database and TVS recommendations are aligned with the following industry standards: IEC 61000-4-2 (system-level ESD), IEC 61000-4-5 (surge), ISO 10605 (automotive ESD), JEDEC JS-001 (component HBM), and ISO 7637-2 (automotive transient). The applicable standard for each signal is shown in the results table.

Version history: Version 2.0 adds Plotly-based interactive charts, a 12-device TVS database, expanded signal patterns covering 22 interface types, JSON export, save/load configuration, and this comprehensive user manual. Version 1.0 was the original standalone reference file at /Ref/esd-protection.html.

2. Quick Start Tutorial

This walkthrough takes approximately 3–5 minutes and produces a complete ESD protection requirements list for your schematic.

Prerequisites

You need a schematic PDF with an embedded text layer. Export the PDF from your EDA tool using File → Export → PDF (not a printer scan). Most modern EDA tools — Altium Designer, KiCad, OrCAD Capture, Eagle — produce text-layer PDFs by default. To verify, open the PDF in Adobe Reader and try to select text. If text selection works, the PDF is compatible with this tool.

Step-by-Step Workflow

  1. Open the tool. Navigate to Tools → ESD Protection Analyzer from the Rising Edge homepage. The page loads with an upload zone and an options panel.
  2. Upload your schematic. Drag your schematic PDF onto the upload zone, or click the zone to browse for it. Files up to 50 MB are accepted. A file information bar confirms the file name and size.
  3. Review analysis options. The default settings (IEC 61000-4-2, Level 4, 3.3 V VDD) suit most consumer electronics designs. Change them if your design targets a different standard (automotive → ISO 10605) or operating voltage.
  4. Run the analysis. Click Run ESD Analysis. Six animated progress steps advance as the tool extracts text, identifies signals, classifies them, and generates recommendations. This typically completes in 2–10 seconds depending on schematic size.
  5. Review the extracted text. After extraction, a preview panel shows the first 3,000 characters of the cleaned schematic text. Download the full text file if you want to verify which content was parsed.
  6. Examine the summary stats. The results card shows the number of signals found, TVS devices recommended, high-speed signals identified, and pages scanned.
  7. Review the requirements table. Scroll down to the ESD Protection Requirements table. Each row represents one signal group with its interface type, key threat, critical ESD property, working voltage, polarity, data rate, applicable standards, surge requirements, and recommended protection part. Use the filter chips to view signals by category.
  8. Review the TVS device cards. Each unique TVS device appears as a card with its manufacturer, package, VRWM, VCLAMP, CLINE, IPP, and an engineering note.
  9. Review the PCB layout guidelines. Six layout guideline cards explain the most critical PCB placement and routing requirements for effective ESD protection.
  10. Export results. Click Export CSV for a spreadsheet, Export JSON for a structured report, or Print report for a formatted document. Use Save config to preserve your analysis options for future sessions.
No signals found? Verify the PDF has a text layer (Adobe Reader → select text). If signals are missing that you know are in your schematic, check that their net names follow standard EDA naming conventions (e.g., USB_DP not DIFF_PLUS_DATA). Custom net names will not be matched by the pattern database.

5. Analysis Options

Step 02 in the workflow. These settings tailor the analysis to your design's target environment and requirements.

ESD Test Standard

IEC 61000-4-2
The most widely used system-level ESD test standard for consumer and industrial electronics. Tests the assembled product, not individual components. Contact and air discharge are both tested. This is the default and appropriate for most products with accessible connectors.
ISO 10605
Automotive variant of IEC 61000-4-2. Higher test levels (up to ±25 kV air discharge) reflecting harsher automotive environments. Use this for any automotive ECU, body control module, or infotainment unit.
JEDEC JS-001
Component-level Human Body Model (HBM). Tests bare IC devices before assembly. Relevant when specifying component-level ESD ratings for your BOM selection, not for system-level compliance.
IEC 61000-4-5
Surge/lightning immunity standard. Tests longer-duration, higher-energy pulses (1.2/50 µs voltage, 8/20 µs current) via AC mains or long cable runs. Use this for Ethernet, RS-485, or outdoor equipment connected to long cable runs.

Severity Level

Applies to IEC 61000-4-2 and ISO 10605. Each level doubles the test voltage:

Level Contact discharge Air discharge Typical application
Level 1 ±0.5 kV ±2 kV Controlled environment, benign use
Level 2 ±1 kV ±4 kV Office, industrial with mild ESD risk
Level 3 ±4 kV ±8 kV General industrial
Level 4 ±8 kV ±15 kV Consumer electronics, harsh environments (default)

Default VDD Rail

Sets the baseline working voltage used when a signal's rail cannot be inferred from its net name. The tool uses this to cross-check that recommended TVS devices have an appropriate VRWM. Common values: 1.8 V (low-power mobile), 3.3 V (general digital IO, default), 5.0 V (USB VBUS, legacy logic).

Include Internal Signals

When set to No (default), the tool skips signals that are typically internal to the PCB (QSPI flash, SDRAM, FMC bus). When set to Yes, these signals are included — useful when internal signals are routed to test points, programming headers, or expansion connectors.

Tip: For a first-pass analysis, keep the default settings (IEC 61000-4-2, Level 4, 3.3 V, external only). Run again with Include internal signals: Yes if your design has exposed test pads on signals that would normally be internal.

7. Charts

ESD Clamping Waveform

An interactive dual-axis Plotly chart showing a model of the IEC 61000-4-2 ESD discharge event over 0–120 ns.

Axis / Trace Description
X-axis Time in nanoseconds (0 to 120 ns)
Left Y-axis (amber) ESD discharge current in amperes
Right Y-axis (blue) Clamped voltage across the TVS device in volts
Amber trace Model of IEC 61000-4-2 contact discharge current pulse. Peak occurs near 1 ns and decays with a 60 ns time constant.
Blue trace Resulting clamped voltage at the TVS terminals. Rises with current toward VCLAMP, then decays as current falls.
Annotation Labels the clamping voltage of the primary recommended TVS device at the IEC 61000-4-2 8 kV peak current.

Engineering interpretation: The blue trace represents the voltage the protected IC pin sees during an ESD event. It must remain below the IC's absolute maximum rating (VABS MAX) at all times. A higher-IPP TVS clamps harder (lower blue trace at peak). A lower VRWM TVS begins clamping earlier but may leak current during normal operation.

Interaction: Hover for exact time/voltage/current values. Drag to zoom into the sub-nanosecond rise time region. Double-click to reset zoom. Click legend items to show/hide individual traces.

TVS Capacitance vs Data Rate

A log-log scatter chart showing each recommended TVS device plotted against the maximum data rate for which it is suitable.

Axis / Trace Description
X-axis Data rate in Mbps (logarithmic)
Y-axis TVS line capacitance CLINE in pF (logarithmic)
Red dashed line Maximum allowable CLINE at each data rate tier. Devices must fall below this line to avoid signal integrity degradation.
Colored circles Each recommended TVS device, labeled with the part number. Position reflects its CLINE and the maximum data rate it is compatible with.

Engineering interpretation: Devices below and to the right of the red limit line are suitable for the corresponding data rate. 10. PCB Layout Guidelines

Six layout guideline cards appear below the TVS recommendations. These guidelines apply universally regardless of which specific TVS devices are used.

Guideline 1 — Place TVS at Connector Edge

The TVS diode must be the first component the ESD pulse encounters when entering the PCB. Place it as close as physically possible to the connector pins — the specification is within 5 mm. Each millimetre of trace between the connector pin and the TVS adds approximately 1 nH of inductance, which increases the effective clamping voltage seen by the IC: Veffective = VCLAMP + L × dI/dt. At IEC 61000-4-2 8 kV, dI/dt exceeds 20 A/ns, so even 1 nH adds 20 V to the effective clamp.

Guideline 2 — Ground Return Path

The ground return path of the TVS device is equally critical as the signal path. A poor ground connection means the TVS cathode floats above true ground during the ESD event, negating the clamping action. Best practices: place at least two vias directly under the TVS ground pad, minimize via inductance by using the largest via that fits, and connect to the ground plane with no narrow trace between the pad and the via.

Guideline 3 — Signal Routing Order

The PCB trace must flow strictly: Connector pin → TVS → Protected IC. Any stub, branch, or via between the connector and TVS allows the ESD pulse to bypass the TVS entirely and reach the IC before clamping occurs. For differential pairs, place the TVS array so both lines enter the array before either line connects to anything else on the PCB.

Guideline 4 — Parasitic Capacitance Budget

The TVS line capacitance (CLINE) adds to the signal trace's total loading. For high-speed interfaces, this creates an impedance discontinuity that causes reflections and degrades the eye diagram. The budget at each speed tier: USB 2.0 HS: < 1 pF total; USB 3.x SS: < 0.1 pF; HDMI 2.0: < 0.15 pF; Gigabit Ethernet: < 2 pF; Fast Ethernet: < 5 pF. Trace parasitics must also be included — a 20 mm trace over a ground plane adds approximately 2 pF.

Guideline 5 — Clamping Voltage Verification

The VCLAMP value on the TVS datasheet is measured at a specified peak current. Verify the VCLAMP at the actual IEC discharge current for your test level — at Level 4 (8 kV), the peak current is approximately 16 A in the contact discharge model. Compare this clamping voltage against the absolute maximum pin voltage of the protected IC. Include the additional voltage due to trace inductance (see Guideline 1). Maintain a ≥ 10% safety margin below VABS MAX.

Guideline 6 — Multi-Stage Protection

For interfaces exposed to IEC 61000-4-5 surge (Ethernet, RS-485, outdoor equipment), a single TVS is insufficient. A two-stage design is required: Stage 1 (primary) — a Gas Discharge Tube (GDT) or Metal Oxide Varistor (MOV) absorbs the bulk surge energy. A series impedance element (ferrite bead, common-mode choke, or resistor) decouples the two stages. Stage 2 (secondary) — a fast TVS diode clamps the residual energy to a safe level. The series impedance element must handle the peak surge current (up to 100 A for an 8/20 µs surge) without saturating or opening.

Export CSV

Downloads a comma-separated values file containing all detected signals with all 13 columns (signal name, component, interface, category, key threat, critical ESD property, VWRK, polarity, data rate, standards, surge requirements, protection part). Downloads a structured JSON report containing: tool name, version, analysis date, schematic filename, page count, analysis options (standard, level, VDD), the complete signals array, and a summary object with signal counts by category. This machine-readable format is useful for integrating ESD analysis into CI/CD pipelines or custom reporting dashboards.

Saves the current analysis options (ESD standard, severity level, default VDD, include internal signals) to a JSON file named esd_analysis_config.json. Load this file in a later session to restore your settings without re-entering them. The saved file is a small text file and can be shared with team members to ensure consistent analysis settings across a project.

Load Configuration

Restores previously saved analysis options from a configuration JSON file. The tool validates the file format before applying settings. Invalid or incompatible files are rejected with an error message.

Print Report

Opens the browser's print dialog with a print-optimized stylesheet applied. Use landscape orientation for best results when printing the results table. The printed report can also be saved as PDF using the browser's "Save as PDF" print destination.

15. Engineering Calculations

All calculations are implemented in js/esd-calculations.js as pure functions exported on window.EsdCalc. Chart waveform models are in js/esd-charts.js.

IEC 61000-4-2 Waveform Model

The discharge current is modelled as a two-exponential function approximating the IEC 61000-4-2 contact discharge waveform: 

I(t) = Ip_fast × (1 − e^(−t/τ_rise)) × e^(−t/τ_fast)
       + Ip_slow × (1 − e^(−t/τ_rise2)) × e^(−t/τ_slow)

Where: τ_rise ≈ 0.7 ns (rise time), τ_fast ≈ 2.5 ns (first peak decay), τ_slow ≈ 60 ns (second peak decay). The peak current at 8 kV contact discharge is approximately 16 A.

Clamping Voltage Model

V(t) is modelled as a simple linear interpolation between VRWM (at zero current) and VCLAMP (at peak current): 

V(t) = V_RWM + (V_CLAMP − V_RWM) × min(1, I(t) / I_peak)

This is a simplified model. Real TVS devices have a nonlinear V-I characteristic that is better characterised by TLP (Transmission Line Pulse) measurements. The actual VCLAMP at intermediate currents can be obtained from the TVS manufacturer's TLP data or application note.

Capacitance Budget Check (Chart)

The maximum allowable line capacitance at each data rate tier is defined empirically from industry practice and is hardcoded in the chart module: 

C_max(f) = 1 / (2π × Z₀ × f × k)

Where Z₀ is the characteristic impedance (typically 50 Ω for RF, 45 Ω for USB HS), f is the signal frequency (data rate / 2 for NRZ), and k is a technology-specific factor (typically 0.1–0.3 to limit the capacitive discontinuity reflection coefficient). The chart uses pre-computed values from industry ESD guidelines (USB-IF, HDMI Forum, IEEE 802.3).

Signal Identification Algorithm

The pattern matching engine (in identifySignals()) iterates through all 22 signal pattern entries in the ESD_SIGNALS database. Each entry has a case-insensitive regular expression. Matches are de-duplicated across patterns (a net name is only reported once even if it matches multiple patterns). The function returns a signal array sorted by detection order in the pattern database.

16. Engineering Concepts

What is ESD?

Electrostatic Discharge (ESD) is the rapid transfer of electrical charge between two objects at different electrostatic potentials. The discharge can occur in nanoseconds. A human body charged to 15 kV (typical from walking across a carpet in dry conditions) can discharge through an IC pin in less than 1 ns. The resulting current pulse exceeds 30 A. Modern CMOS IC gate oxides are only 2–5 nanometres thick and can rupture at junction voltages as low as 2–3 V. Without external ESD protection, a single discharge can permanently destroy a multi-hundred-dollar IC.

Human Body Model (HBM)

The HBM simulates discharge from a person to a device. It models the human body as a 100 pF capacitor in series with a 1.5 kΩ resistor. The resulting current pulse has a 10 ns rise time and a 150 ns decay. HBM is used for component-level testing (JEDEC JS-001) and typically rated in kV (e.g., "HBM 2 kV"). This is different from IEC 61000-4-2, which is a system-level test — the same component that passes HBM 8 kV at the component level may still fail IEC 61000-4-2 8 kV at the system level due to board parasitics and interconnect inductance.

Charged Device Model (CDM)

CDM simulates an IC that has become charged during handling (from friction with packaging materials) and then discharges through a pin making contact with a grounded surface. CDM pulses are much faster than HBM — sub-nanosecond rise time — and cause different failure modes (gate oxide damage from current flowing in the reverse direction through protection structures). CDM is primarily a concern during manufacturing and assembly, not during product use.

IEC 61000-4-2

The most widely applied ESD standard for finished products. Tests the assembled system rather than individual components. The contact discharge model is more aggressive than HBM (different waveform, higher dI/dt). Level 4 contact discharge (±8 kV) produces a peak current of approximately 16 A with a rise time < 1 ns. The system must continue to function (or recover automatically) after the specified number of discharges at each level.

TVS Diode Operation

A Transient Voltage Suppressor (TVS) diode is a specially optimised Zener diode designed to clamp voltage spikes. Below the breakdown voltage (VBR ≈ VRWM × 1.1), the TVS is essentially an open circuit — it does not load the signal. Above VBR, it enters avalanche breakdown and clamps the voltage to VCLAMP while absorbing the discharge energy as heat. The TVS must absorb the entire ESD energy: E = ½ × C × V² (for HBM, C = 100 pF, V = test voltage). Modern TVS devices achieve clamping in < 1 ps, far faster than the ESD event rise time.

Dynamic Resistance

During clamping, the TVS has a dynamic resistance RDYN = (VCLAMP − VBR) / IPP. A lower RDYN means a harder clamp — the clamping voltage increases less with current. For high-energy ESD, low RDYN is critical to keep VCLAMP below the IC's VABS MAX. Rail-clamp TVS arrays (like the USBLC6 series) use a diode-network topology that achieves lower RDYN than single-junction Zener-based TVS devices.

Bidirectional vs Unidirectional TVS

Bidirectional (bipolar) TVS devices clamp voltage in both polarities — above the positive threshold and below ground. They are required for any AC signal, differential signal, or IO pin that can go negative relative to ground. Unidirectional (unipolar) TVS devices only clamp in the positive direction (above VBR). They are suitable for DC power rails, VBUS, and any signal that never goes below ground. Unidirectional devices typically have lower VCLAMP for the same VRWM, making them preferable for power lines.

Rail-to-Rail TVS Arrays

Modern high-speed interface TVS devices (e.g., USBLC6-2SC6 for USB, TPD4E004 for HDMI) use a rail-to-rail topology: each protected signal pin has two diodes — one connecting to the power rail (anode to signal, cathode to VCC) and one connecting to ground (anode to GND, cathode to signal). A Zener or avalanche element clamps the VCC rail during positive ESD events. This topology achieves very low RDYN, extremely low CLINE (the diodes are very small), and automatic scaling — the clamp voltage adapts to the supply rail voltage.

19. PCB Layout Rules

Priority rules (must follow)

  • TVS must be the first component after the connector. Measured from the connector pin to the nearest TVS pad: < 5 mm on the signal layer.
  • No trace branches, stubs, vias, or other components between the connector pin and the TVS input pad.
  • TVS ground pad must connect to the ground plane with ≥ 2 vias directly under or adjacent to the pad.
  • For differential pairs, place a balanced TVS array (both lines into the same device before splitting).

Important rules (should follow)

  • Keep TVS devices on the same PCB layer as the connector to avoid via inductance in the ESD current path.
  • Use the widest feasible trace between connector and TVS to minimize trace inductance (wider = lower inductance up to the plane resonance point).
  • Add a dedicated ESD ground pour or flood fill around the TVS devices, connected to the main ground plane with multiple vias at regular intervals.
  • For RS-485 and Ethernet requiring multi-stage protection, place the GDT/MOV immediately after the connector, the series impedance next, and the secondary TVS before the transceiver IC.

Rules for high-speed interfaces

  • After TVS, continue the differential pair with controlled impedance routing (typically 90 Ω differential for USB, 100 Ω for HDMI).
  • Keep TVS as close to the connector as possible to minimize the length of uncontrolled-impedance trace before the TVS.
  • Match the electrical length of both lines in a differential pair through the TVS pads (symmetric layout).
  • For USB 3.x (CLINE < 0.1 pF), evaluate whether any TVS device meets the budget — PESD0402 or ESD008-P2-02VH are the best available options.

21. Frequently Asked Questions

Which TVS diode should I choose for USB 2.0?

The USBLC6-2SC6 (STMicroelectronics) is the industry standard recommendation for USB 2.0 High Speed. It provides 0.5 pF CLINE per data line, covers both D+ and D−, and includes a unidirectional VBUS clamp — all in a single SOT-23-6 package. It is specifically characterised for IEC 61000-4-2 Level 4 compliance. Do not use a generic TVS for USB HS — even 2 pF will cause eye closure at 480 Mbps.

Why did the tool find no signals in my schematic?

The most common cause is a rasterized (image-only) PDF. Export the PDF from your EDA tool using the native PDF export function, not by printing and scanning. A secondary cause is non-standard net naming. If your nets use names like DIFF_USB_P instead of USB_DP, they will not match the pattern database. Consider adding standard alias net labels in your schematic. Check the extracted text preview — if it shows mostly spaces and garbled characters, the PDF has no readable text layer.

Why is the clamp voltage too high?

VCLAMP is measured at the peak IEC discharge current. If the TVS is too far from the connector, trace inductance adds to the effective clamp. Reduce the connector-to-TVS trace length. If the TVS itself has inherently high VCLAMP, choose a device with lower RDYN or lower VBR. Check that the TVS ground has very low inductance — a poor ground is often the hidden cause of high effective VCLAMP.

Should I use ferrite beads for ESD protection?

Ferrite beads are useful as decoupling elements in multi-stage protection, not as primary ESD protectors. Placed as the series impedance between a primary stage (GDT/MOV) and a secondary stage (TVS), they force the primary stage to absorb more of the surge energy. Ferrite beads alone cannot protect against fast ESD — they saturate at the high currents involved. For single-stage protection, a TVS alone is the correct approach.

How can I improve ESD immunity without adding components?

PCB layout improvements often provide more benefit than component upgrades: shorten the connector-to-TVS trace, add more ground vias under the TVS, improve the continuity of the ground plane under ESD current paths, and ensure the chassis connection (if present) is a short, low-inductance bond. These layout changes can reduce effective VCLAMP by 10–30% without any BOM changes.

What causes secondary ESD failures after the TVS is installed?

Common causes: 1) TVS is too far from the connector and trace inductance raises the effective clamp above VABS MAX. 2) Ground return path is inadequate — the TVS clamps but current flows back through a high-inductance path, raising the IC's ground reference transiently. 3) Differential signals — only one line of a pair is protected, causing common-mode imbalance that stresses the other line. 4) TVS was damaged during assembly by ESD before being soldered (CDM). Always use ESD-safe handling when placing TVS devices on the assembly line.

Do I need different TVS devices for USB-C?

Yes. USB-C adds CC lines (for power delivery negotiation), VCONN, SBU1/SBU2 (sideband use), and the full USB 3.2 or USB4 SuperSpeed differential pairs in addition to the USB 2.0 D+/D− lines. Each group has different VRWM and CLINE requirements. Dedicated USB-C ESD protection ICs (e.g., TUSB546, HD3SS460) combine multiple protection functions. Do not use a single USBLC6-2SC6 for a full USB-C connector — it covers only D+/D−.

23. Glossary

Avalanche breakdown
The mechanism by which a reverse-biased diode begins conducting at a specific voltage (VBR). In a TVS, this is the designed-in clamping mechanism.
Bipolar TVS
A TVS that clamps in both positive and negative polarities. Required for any signal that can swing below ground.
CLINE
Line capacitance of the TVS device — the parasitic capacitance that loads the signal. The primary signal integrity constraint for high-speed interface protection.
CDM
Charged Device Model. An ESD model simulating a charged IC package discharging through a pin during handling. Characterized by sub-nanosecond rise time and low total energy.
Clamping voltage (VCLAMP)
The voltage across the TVS at the specified peak pulse current. The voltage seen by the protected IC during an ESD event.
Contact discharge
IEC 61000-4-2 test mode where the discharge electrode contacts the DUT surface before the trigger is activated. More repeatable and typically more stressful than air discharge.
Dynamic resistance (RDYN)
The incremental resistance of the TVS during clamping: RDYN = ΔV/ΔI. Lower RDYN produces a harder, flatter clamp — the voltage increases less with increasing current.
ESD
Electrostatic Discharge. Rapid transfer of electrostatic charge between two objects at different potentials.
GDT
Gas Discharge Tube. A primary surge protection device that triggers at a high voltage (~300V) and then crowbars to near-zero voltage. High surge energy capacity. Not suitable as the sole protection device due to high trigger voltage.
HBM
Human Body Model. An ESD discharge model simulating a charged person (100 pF, 1.5 kΩ). Used in JEDEC JS-001 for component testing and IEC 61000-4-2 for system testing (with different waveform parameters).
IPP
Peak pulse current handling capability of a TVS device. Must exceed the peak ESD discharge current for the applied test level.
MOV
Metal Oxide Varistor. A voltage-dependent resistor used as primary surge protection. High capacitance and asymmetric aging characteristics make it unsuitable for signal lines.
RDYN
See Dynamic resistance.
Rail-to-rail TVS
A TVS topology using steering diodes to a power rail (VCC and GND), with a Zener element clamping the power rail. Achieves very low CLINE and RDYN. Used in USBLC6 series, TPD4E004, and similar ICs.
TLP
Transmission Line Pulse. A characterization technique that measures TVS V-I characteristics at ESD-realistic pulse widths (typically 100 ns). TLP data is more accurate for ESD design than DC characterization or standard IEC pulse data.
TVS
Transient Voltage Suppressor. A protection device that clamps transient voltages by entering avalanche breakdown within picoseconds of the trigger voltage being exceeded.
Unipolar TVS
A TVS that clamps only in the positive direction. Suitable for DC power rails and signals that never go negative. Typically has a lower VCLAMP for the same VRWM compared to a bipolar device.
VABS MAX
Absolute maximum voltage rating of an IC pin. The voltage across the TVS during an ESD event (VCLAMP including trace inductance effects) must not exceed this value.
VBR
Breakdown voltage. The voltage at which the TVS begins conducting significantly (measured at 1 mA by convention). Approximately 10% above VRWM for most devices.
VCLAMP
See Clamping voltage.
VRWM
Reverse Working Voltage (also called standoff voltage). The maximum reverse voltage the TVS can sustain without conducting. Must be set above the signal's DC operating voltage plus worst-case AC swing.
