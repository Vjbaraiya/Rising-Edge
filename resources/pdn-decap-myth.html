Overview

How Anti-Resonance Peaks Form

Every real capacitor is an RLC circuit: capacitance C in series with ESR (equivalent series resistance) and ESL (equivalent series inductance). Below its self-resonant frequency (SRF), it behaves as a capacitor. Above SRF, ESL dominates and it behaves as an inductor.

When you place two capacitors with different SRFs in parallel — say a 100 µF bulk electrolytic (SRF ≈ 500 kHz) and a 100 nF ceramic (SRF ≈ 50 MHz) — a frequency region exists between the two SRFs where the small cap looks inductive and the large cap looks capacitive. This is a textbook LC parallel resonance: an anti-resonance peak where impedance rises sharply instead of falling.

The paradox: Adding more 100 nF ceramics pushes their combined SRF higher (lower ESL in parallel) — but does nothing to suppress the anti-resonance with the bulk cap. The peak frequency shifts but the amplitude stays high.
Design principle: The goal is a PDN impedance profile that stays below Z_target across the entire frequency range of interest — not minimum total capacitance. A well-designed 3-tier hierarchy with damping outperforms a bag of mismatched caps.

01

Self-Resonant Frequency of a Real Capacitor

Every capacitor has an SRF above which it becomes an inductor — the fundamental source of anti-resonance

Real capacitor model

A real capacitor is not an ideal capacitor. It is an RLC series circuit: C (the intended capacitance) in series with ESR (equivalent series resistance — PCB traces, lead resistance, dielectric losses) and ESL (equivalent series inductance — from lead geometry, via path, package inductance).

Self-resonant frequency

f_SRF = 1 / (2π × √(ESL × C))

|Z| at SRF = ESR (minimum, purely resistive)
Below f_SRF: |Z| = 1/(2πfC) — capacitive
Above f_SRF: |Z| = 2πf × ESL — inductive

Typical SRF values

TypeValueTypical SRF
Electrolytic (thru-hole)100 µF200–500 kHz
MLCC 080510 µF2–5 MHz
MLCC 0402100 nF30–80 MHz
MLCC 020110 nF200–500 MHz
MLCC 010051 nF1–3 GHz

What determines ESL?

  • Package geometry: Smaller packages have lower ESL — 0201 ≈ 0.3 nH; 0402 ≈ 0.5 nH; 0805 ≈ 1.0 nH
  • PCB via inductance: Each via adds ~0.5–1 nH. Vias in parallel (interdigitated caps) reduce this
  • Pad geometry: Long pad traces add inductance; minimize trace length between cap and via
  • Layer transition: Caps on the top layer supplying a via to an inner power plane add the via inductance in series

Impedance above SRF

Above the SRF, a 100 nF capacitor with 0.5 nH ESL behaves like a 0.5 nH inductor. At 500 MHz:

|Z| = 2π × 500×10⁶ × 0.5×10⁻⁹ = 1.57 Ω

vs Z_target ≈ 5–50 mΩ for a typical SoC PDN
→ 30–300× above target at 500 MHz

This is why close-in 01005 caps and on-die capacitance are needed to control PDN impedance above 100 MHz

02

Anti-Resonance Between Parallel Capacitors

The impedance peak between two capacitors' SRFs — adding caps of the wrong value makes this worse

How anti-resonance forms

Consider two capacitors in parallel: C1 (large, low SRF) and C2 (small, high SRF).

  • Below f_SRF1: Both capacitive. Z falls with frequency. ✓
  • Between f_SRF1 and f_SRF2: C1 looks inductive (above its SRF), C2 still capacitive → parallel LC resonance → Z peaks. ✗
  • Above f_SRF2: Both inductive. Z rises with frequency. ✗

Anti-resonance frequency

f_anti ≈ 1 / (2π × √(ESL₁ × C₂))

when C₁ >> C₂ (dominant case)

Example: ESL₁ = 5 nH (100µF electrolytic)
C₂ = 100 nF (ceramic)
f_anti ≈ 1/(2π×√(5×10⁻⁹×100×10⁻⁹))
≈ 7.1 MHz

Anti-resonance peak amplitude

Z_peak ≈ √(ESL₁ / C₂)

ESL₁ = 5 nH, C₂ = 100 nF:
Z_peak ≈ √(5×10⁻⁹ / 100×10⁻⁹) = √0.05 = 0.224 Ω

If Z_target = 10 mΩ, this peak is 22× target!

Adding more ceramics makes it worse

Adding 10× 100 nF ceramics in parallel gives C2_total = 1 µF and ESL2_total = 0.05 nH (10 in parallel). The new anti-resonance frequency:

f_anti_new ≈ 1/(2π×√(5×10⁻⁹×1×10⁻⁶)) ≈ 2.25 MHz
Z_peak_new ≈ √(5×10⁻⁹/1×10⁻⁶) = 0.071 Ω

Still 7× above target — only frequency shifted
  • Adding more ceramics shifts the anti-resonance to lower frequency but does not suppress it
  • The only way to suppress the peak is to add damping (ESR or RC network)

Hierarchical PDN Design

Each capacitor tier targets a frequency decade — the handover between tiers must be designed, not hoped for

PDN hierarchy tiers

Tier 1 — Voltage Regulator / Bulk (DC–100 kHz) 10–10,000 µF aluminium electrolytic or polymer

Provides DC accuracy and handles slow load steps (µs timescale). Located near the regulator output. ESL 5–50 nH limits effectiveness above ~1 MHz.

Tier 2 — Mid-Field Ceramic (100 kHz–10 MHz) 1–47 µF MLCC 0402–0603, placed 5–30 mm from IC

Handles moderate-speed transients. Key tier for anti-resonance management. SRF typically 1–10 MHz. Provides charge for ns-to-µs events.

Tier 3 — Close-In Ceramic (10 MHz–1 GHz) 10–100 nF MLCC 0201/01005 within 1–5 mm of IC

Handles fast transients. Placement dominates — inductance of each mounting via limits effectiveness. SRF 50–500 MHz.

Tier 4 — On-Die Capacitance (1 GHz+) 1–100 µF integrated into IC die or package

Fastest tier. Sub-nH ESL. Handles edge-rate transients that no PCB capacitor can reach in time. Cannot be added by the PCB designer.

The tier handover problem

Each tier hands off to the next at a frequency where one tier's capacitance becomes ineffective (above its SRF) and the next tier takes over. The anti-resonance peaks occur precisely at these handover frequencies.

Good tier design rules

  • Keep the SRF ratio between adjacent tiers <10× to limit anti-resonance amplitude
  • Use ESR to damp the handover: a tantalum or polymer cap in Tier 1 has natural ESR that suppresses the bulk-to-ceramic anti-resonance
  • Avoid mixing widely different capacitor values in the same tier (e.g., 1 µF and 100 nF MLCC in the same Tier 3 placement)
  • Simulate the PDN impedance with SPICE or a PDN analyser before finalising the BOM

Interplane capacitance (bonus tier)

A pair of adjacent power/ground planes has distributed capacitance: C_plane ≈ ε₀×εᵣ×A / d. For a 100×100 mm plane pair at 0.1 mm separation with εᵣ=4.2:

C_plane ≈ 8.85×10⁻¹²×4.2×0.01/0.0001 ≈ 3.7 nF

Small, but effective above 1 GHz with near-zero ESL — fills the gap between Tier 3 ceramics and on-die capacitance

04

Mounting Inductance — Why Placement Beats Value Above 100 MHz

A 10 nF cap 10 mm from the IC is no better than a 100 nF cap 1 mm away at 500 MHz

Via inductance model

The total inductance from a PCB capacitor to the IC power pin includes: capacitor package ESL + pad-to-via trace inductance + via inductance + power plane spreading inductance.

L_via ≈ 0.2×h×[ln(4h/d)−1] nH
h = via length (mm), d = drill diameter (mm)

h=1.6 mm (standard PCB), d=0.3 mm:
L_via ≈ 0.2×1.6×[ln(21.3)−1] ≈ 0.9 nH per via

Effective SRF with mounting inductance

L_total = L_pkg + L_via + L_plane
f_SRF_effective = 1/(2π×√(L_total × C))

100 nF cap, L_pkg=0.5 nH, L_via=0.9 nH:
L_total = 1.4 nH
f_SRF_eff = 13.4 MHz (vs 50 MHz for ideal)

Distance vs value trade-off

Routing trace inductance from cap pad to via: approximately 0.7 nH per mm for a 0.2 mm wide trace on 0.1 mm dielectric.

  • A cap 5 mm from the via adds ~3.5 nH of trace inductance — more than the via itself
  • Moving a 100 nF cap from 5 mm to 0.5 mm from the IC power vias reduces L_trace from ~3.5 nH to ~0.35 nH — equivalent to adding 10 caps in parallel from 5 mm away
  • For frequencies above 100 MHz, distance matters far more than capacitor value

Best placement practices

  • Place 01005 or 0201 caps directly adjacent to IC power/ground pin pairs — minimise via-to-via distance
  • Use power/ground via pairs with caps between them (interdigitated layout) to minimise loop area
  • Avoid long pad traces: the cap body should be as close to the via as the annular ring allows
  • Caps on the bottom layer under a BGA are more effective than top-layer caps at the edge of the package

Damping Strategies for Anti-Resonance Suppression

Series resistance, RC damper networks, and optimal ESR — three ways to kill the peak

Why anti-resonance peaks are sharp

An ideal capacitor has zero ESR. In a parallel resonance, the Q factor Q = 1/ESR × √(L/C). With low ESR ceramics (ESR ≈ 1–10 mΩ) and low ESL (0.5 nH) with 100 nF capacitance:

Q = (1/0.005) × √(0.5×10⁻⁹/100×10⁻⁹)
= 200 × 0.071 = 14.2

High-Q → sharp, tall peak

Strategy 1: Optimal ESR

The anti-resonance peak is critically damped when the ESR of the larger cap equals the characteristic impedance of the parallel LC circuit:

R_optimal ≈ √(ESL₁ / C₂)

ESL₁=5 nH, C₂=100 nF:
R_opt ≈ √(5e-9/100e-9) = 0.224 Ω

Use a tantalum or polymer cap with this ESR

Strategy 2: RC damper network

Add an RC series network in parallel with the bulk cap: R_damp in series with C_damp. The capacitor presents low impedance at the anti-resonance frequency while the resistor provides damping. C_damp ≈ 3–10× the bulk cap value; R_damp ≈ √(L_bulk/C_damp).

  • C_damp is not a decoupling capacitor — it only provides a damping path
  • R_damp will dissipate power at DC: P = (V_ripple)²/R_damp — use resistors rated accordingly
  • The RC values need tuning via simulation — a 3:1 ratio change in R_damp changes peak height by ~10 dB

Strategy 3: Same-family capacitors

The simplest mitigation: replace a wide-SRF mix with capacitors from the same family (same value, same package). N identical capacitors in parallel have the same SRF as one — no anti-resonance between them. The combined impedance is just 1/N × the single-cap impedance.

N × identical caps: Z_total = Z_single / N
f_SRF_total = f_SRF_single (unchanged)
No inter-cap anti-resonance ✓

Real-World Anti-Resonance Failures

FPGA Board

Xilinx Artix-7 — random configuration failures

Designer added 20× 100 nF ceramics to the VCCINT rail after seeing minor noise. Anti-resonance peak between the ceramics and 470 µF polymer bulk cap shifted from 8 MHz to 3.5 MHz — directly in the range of the configuration clock's transient spectrum. Failed rate increased. Removing 15 caps and adding a 100 mΩ damping resistor in series with the bulk cap fixed it.

RF SoC

Spurious harmonics on VCO supply

PCB had 4 different cap values (100 nF, 10 nF, 1 nF, 100 pF) on the VCO supply — standard "copy from datasheet" decoupling. Three anti-resonance peaks at 15 MHz, 150 MHz, and 1.2 GHz appeared as spurs in the VCO output spectrum. Replacing with 5× identical 100 nF 0201 caps eliminated all three peaks.

High-Speed ADC

SFDR degraded at specific frequencies

16-bit ADC showed 12 dB SFDR degradation at input frequencies of 4.2 MHz and 41 MHz — exactly the anti-resonance frequencies of the analogue supply PDN. The supply had 100 µF electrolytic + 1 µF + 100 nF ceramics. Adding a 220 mΩ resistor in series with the electrolytic and removing the 1 µF cap reduced anti-resonance to below the noise floor.

Motor Controller

Gate driver supply oscillation at partial load

The 15 V gate driver supply showed 400 mV oscillation at 12 MHz only when switching at 50% duty. Analysis: the PDN anti-resonance peak was at 12 MHz — the same as the 200 kHz switching frequency × 60th harmonic. Fixed by adding a 1 Ω + 10 µF RC damper across the 4.7 µF ceramic, reducing the Q of the peak from 18 to 2.

Common Mistakes

Five Ways PDN Decoupling Goes Wrong

M1

Copying decoupling from datasheets without simulating

Symptom: Datasheet says "add 100 nF, 10 nF, 1 nF" — board has noise at three specific frequencies

Datasheets specify capacitor values for a reference board impedance — not your stackup, not your mounting geometry, not your current draw. Three different cap values create two anti-resonance peaks at the handover frequencies.

Fix: Simulate the PDN with your actual ESL values (package + mounting). Then replace mismatched values with same-family caps in the correct tiers, adding damping at the bulk-to-ceramic handover.
M2

Adding more caps to fix noise without measuring the PDN first

Symptom: Noise at 8 MHz; engineer adds 10 more 100 nF caps; noise moves to 3 MHz and gets worse

Adding identical caps in parallel shifts the combined SRF upward (lowers combined ESL), moving the anti-resonance peak lower in frequency. If the noise frequency is below the new anti-resonance, the situation improves — but if it was already at the bulk-to-ceramic boundary, adding more ceramics shifts the peak toward the noise frequency.

Fix: Measure PDN impedance with a VNA across the power/ground pins before changing the BOM. Identify which anti-resonance peak is causing the noise. Then damp that peak rather than adding capacitance.
M3

Placing all decoupling caps at the board edge "for convenience"

Symptom: PDN simulation looks perfect; measured board noise is 10× worse than simulated

Simulation used the capacitor's datasheet ESL. Real ESL includes PCB trace inductance (0.7 nH/mm) and via inductance (~0.9 nH each). A cap 10 mm from the IC has ~7 nH of additional trace inductance — shifting its effective SRF from 50 MHz down to 8 MHz. The cap is useless for its intended frequency range.

Fix: Include mounting inductance in the simulation. Use the rule: total L = ESL_pkg + 0.9 nH per via + 0.7 nH per mm of trace. Place Tier 3 caps within 1–2 mm of IC power pins, not at the board edge.
M4

Using X5R/X7R capacitors whose value drops at operating voltage

Symptom: 10 µF cap on the BOM; measured on the board at operating voltage: 2.5 µF — anti-resonance shifted

X5R/X7R MLCC capacitors lose 50–80% of their rated capacitance at DC bias. A 10 µF 0402 X5R rated at 6.3 V may measure only 2 µF at 3.3 V operating bias. This shifts the SRF upward and the anti-resonance frequency — the PDN simulation (done at zero bias) is wrong.

Fix: Derate capacitors by 2–3× for X5R/X7R parts at operating voltage. Simulate the PDN with the derated value. Alternatively, use C0G/NP0 dielectric (no voltage dependence) for critical decoupling on voltage-sensitive rails.
M5

Ignoring the inductor in the VR output filter as a PDN element

Symptom: PDN has a resonance peak at VR switching frequency × N

The voltage regulator output LC filter (e.g., 1 µH output inductor + 470 µF bulk cap) forms a series resonance at f = 1/(2π×√(L×C)) ≈ 232 kHz. The VR output impedance peaks at this frequency. If the bulk cap's SRF is also near 232 kHz, the resonance is poorly damped and the PDN impedance spike can trigger oscillation.

Fix: Model the VR output inductor in the PDN simulation. Ensure the bulk cap's ESR provides adequate damping of the LC resonance: ESR_optimal = √(L_VR / C_bulk). Polymer capacitors (ESR ~30–100 mΩ) are naturally better damped than ceramics (ESR ~1–5 mΩ) in this tier.
Interactive Decision Tree

What's Causing My PDN Problem?

How does the power supply noise appear?

Does the noise appear at the anti-resonance frequency (between two cap SRFs)?

Does the noise floor change when you add or remove capacitors?

How fast is the load step?

→ Anti-resonance between bulk and ceramic tiers

Measure PDN impedance with a VNA to confirm the peak frequency and amplitude. Add a damping resistor in series with the bulk capacitor (start with R = √(ESL_bulk/C_ceramic)). Alternatively, replace the bulk capacitor with a polymer type that has natural ESR in the right range. Do not add more ceramics — this shifts the peak frequency but does not reduce the amplitude.

→ VR switching noise coupling into supply

The VR output filter LC resonance may not be adequately damped. Check that the bulk cap's ESR ≥ √(L_VR_inductor / C_bulk). If using ceramic bulk caps, add a series resistor of 50–200 mΩ with the bulk cap or switch to polymer/tantalum with higher natural ESR. Add a ferrite bead between VR output and IC supply pins if high-frequency coupling is severe.

→ Insufficient mid-frequency decoupling (PDN impedance too high in a band)

Simulate the PDN impedance profile. Find the frequency band where Z > Z_target. Add capacitance at the tier that covers that band — but add same-value caps within a tier to avoid creating new anti-resonance peaks. If the gap is between existing tiers, add a Tier 2 mid-field capacitor tier at the right SRF.

→ Likely not a PDN capacitance issue — check for crosstalk or EMI

If PDN cap changes don't affect the noise, the problem is not a PDN impedance issue. Check for: magnetic coupling from an adjacent switching converter, common-mode noise from interface cables, or a layout ground return path problem (ground bounce). Measure the noise source with a current probe on the power inductor to distinguish switching noise from load-side noise.

→ On-die or package decoupling is insufficient

Sub-10 ns transients travel at most ~25 mm before the PCB capacitors can respond. Only on-die capacitance and package capacitors respond fast enough. PCB caps cannot help. If the IC lacks sufficient on-die capacitance and the problem is critical, consider a chip-scale package decoupling capacitor (e.g., Murata IPD cap mounted under the IC) or a lower-inductance via stack to the closest Tier 3 cap.

→ Tier 3 close-in ceramic capacitance is insufficient or poorly placed

Transients in the 10 ns – 1 µs range are handled by Tier 3 ceramics (0201/01005 near the IC). Check: (1) are the caps within 1–2 mm of the IC power vias? (2) Is the total capacitance at least 10× the required charge (Q = ΔI × Δt / ΔV)? (3) Are the via pairs interdigitated to minimise loop inductance? Increase Tier 3 capacitance using additional same-value caps rather than larger values.

→ Bulk capacitance or VR bandwidth is insufficient

Slow transients (>1 µs) are handled by bulk capacitors and the voltage regulator bandwidth. Increase bulk capacitance (same value in parallel to avoid anti-resonance), or increase VR bandwidth by reducing the output inductor value. A VR with a 100 µs response time cannot handle a 10 µs load step — the bulk cap must supply the charge during the VR response delay.

Engineering Calculation Exercise

Scenario — FPGA core supply PDN

A 1.0 V FPGA core supply has a maximum transient current of 8 A and requires voltage ripple ≤ 40 mV. The current PDN has: one 220 µF aluminium electrolytic (ESL = 15 nH, ESR = 80 mΩ) and five 100 nF MLCC 0402 ceramics (ESL = 0.5 nH each, ESR = 5 mΩ each).

  • 1. Calculate Z_target for this supply
  • 2. Calculate the SRF of the bulk electrolytic
  • 3. Calculate the anti-resonance peak frequency and amplitude between the bulk and the ceramic combination
  • 4. Is the anti-resonance peak a problem? What is the recommended damping resistor?

Solution

1. Z_target:

Z_target = ΔV / ΔI = 40 mV / 8 A = 5 mΩ
This is a very tight target. Any anti-resonance peak above 5 mΩ is a problem.
2. SRF of 220 µF electrolytic (ESL = 15 nH):

f_SRF1 = 1/(2π×√(15×10⁻⁹ × 220×10⁻⁶))
= 1/(2π×√(3.3×10⁻¹²))
= 1/(2π×1.817×10⁻⁶)
f_SRF1 ≈ 87.6 kHz
3. Anti-resonance between bulk and 5× ceramics in parallel:

Combined ceramic: C₂_total = 5 × 100 nF = 500 nF
Combined ESL₂ = 0.5 nH / 5 = 0.1 nH

Anti-resonance frequency:
f_anti ≈ 1/(2π×√(ESL₁ × C₂_total))
= 1/(2π×√(15×10⁻⁹ × 500×10⁻⁹))
= 1/(2π×√(7.5×10⁻¹⁵))
= 1/(2π×86.6×10⁻⁹)
f_anti ≈ 1.84 MHz

Peak impedance estimate:
Z_peak ≈ √(ESL₁/C₂_total) = √(15×10⁻⁹ / 500×10⁻⁹) = √0.03
Z_peak ≈ 173 mΩ
4. Is 173 mΩ a problem?

Z_target = 5 mΩ. Anti-resonance peak = 173 mΩ = 34× above target!
Yes — this will cause significant core voltage noise at ~1.84 MHz.

Required damping resistor:
R_opt ≈ √(ESL₁/C₂_total) = 173 mΩ (same formula as Z_peak)

But ESR₁ = 80 mΩ is already present. The deficit is: 173 − 80 = 93 mΩ additional

Add a 100 mΩ resistor in series with the bulk electrolytic, OR replace it with a 220 µF polymer capacitor with ESR ≈ 100–200 mΩ (natural damping). This reduces the peak from 173 mΩ to approximately 10–20 mΩ — still above Z_target, so additional Tier 2 mid-field ceramics (same value, 4.7 µF) should be added to bridge the impedance gap between the tiers.
The bulk electrolytic's own ESR (80 mΩ) provides some damping — the undamped formula overestimates the peak. Real simulation would show a peak of roughly 80–100 mΩ before adding the resistor. But even 80 mΩ is 16× Z_target — still a serious problem.
