Overview

The Real SI Parameter: Edge Rate

Signal integrity engineers have a phrase: "It's not the clock that matters — it's the edge." A waveform's ability to excite transmission line effects, reflections, ringing, and crosstalk is determined almost entirely by its rise and fall time (tr/tf — the edge transition time, conventionally measured 10–90% of the voltage swing), not by how many times per second it transitions. Throughout this article, "rise time" implies fall time as well — real drivers rarely have identical rise and fall edges, and both must be checked.

Modern logic families (CMOS, LVDS, LVCMOS) have rise times of 1–5 ns regardless of clock frequency. A 1 MHz clock with a 2 ns edge and a 100 MHz clock with the same 2 ns edge excite the same transmission-line behaviour, reflections, and ringing on a given trace — that part is edge-rate-only. Their EMI signatures are related but not identical: edge rate sets the same spectral envelope (bandwidth and rolloff) for both, but clock frequency still sets the harmonic spacing, so the 100 MHz clock packs far more harmonic lines under that envelope and radiates more average power. In short — the transmission-line behaviour depends on edge transition rather than repetition frequency, but repetition frequency still shapes the emissions spectrum.

Common misconception: "Our clock is only 10 MHz, so we don't need to worry about transmission lines or termination." In practice, a standard CMOS output at 10 MHz has a ~2 ns rise time — creating an effective bandwidth of ~175 MHz and requiring controlled impedance and termination for traces longer than roughly 56 mm on typical FR4 microstrip (see the critical-length calculation below).
Rule of thumb: BW ≈ 0.35 / tr  (single-pole Gaussian approximation, 10–90% rise time). A 1 ns rise time has ~350 MHz of equivalent signal bandwidth — independent of clock speed. Other references use 0.45 or 0.5 depending on waveform assumptions (see Concept Card 01); this article uses 0.35 throughout for first-order estimation.

01

Rise/Fall Time Defines Bandwidth

The spectrum of a digital signal is set by tr/tf, not fclk

The Fourier argument

A digital signal is not truly band-limited — a periodic trapezoidal waveform has an infinite harmonic series. What we call "bandwidth" here is really the equivalent signal bandwidth: the frequency above which harmonic content falls off fast enough to be considered negligible for most SI/EMI purposes. For a trapezoidal waveform with rise time tr, the spectral envelope rolls off at −20 dB/decade above f1 = 1/(π·τ) (τ = pulse width), then at −40 dB/decade above f2 = 1/(π·tr). The clock frequency only sets the spacing between the harmonic lines under that envelope — not the envelope's shape or its highest-amplitude corner.

Key formula

BW−3dB ≈ 0.35 / tr

This 0.35 constant is a single-pole Gaussian approximation of the −3 dB corner for a 10–90% rise time. It is a convention, not a universal law — different references use different constants depending on waveform model and rise-time definition: 0.35 (Gaussian, used throughout this article), 0.45 (some oscilloscope/TDR literature), or fknee = 0.5 / tr (a simpler linear-ramp model). All three are first-order estimates — treat the result as "approximately this frequency and above," not an exact cutoff.

Why clock frequency is irrelevant to bandwidth

  • A 1 MHz clock with 1 ns edges has identical spectral width to a 100 MHz clock with 1 ns edges
  • The 100 MHz clock has more harmonics (spaced 100 MHz apart), but same rolloff corner
  • Transmission lines, capacitors, and antennas respond to signal bandwidth, not repetition rate

Implication for PCB design

  • Always calculate SI requirements from tr, not fclk
  • Check driver datasheet for rise/fall time — often independent of frequency
  • Slowing edge rate (series resistor or RC filter) is the most effective SI fix
02

Critical Trace Length

Once propagation delay exceeds ~tr/6, a trace behaves as a transmission line

The transmission line threshold

The primary threshold is defined in terms of time, not distance: a trace must be treated as a transmission line once its one-way propagation delay (TD) becomes a significant fraction of the signal's rise time — conventionally, once TD > tr / 6. Below that, the whole trace can be approximated as a single lumped node; above it, different points on the trace see meaningfully different voltages at the same instant, and reflections must be considered.

Converting that time-domain threshold into a physical trace length using the board's propagation delay per unit length (TPD) gives the familiar "λ/6" critical-length rule — so called because TD = tr/6 corresponds to roughly one-sixth of a wavelength at the signal's equivalent bandwidth. The rule is a distance shorthand for the underlying delay comparison, not an independent wavelength criterion:

Lcrit = tr / (6 × TPD)

TPD = propagation delay per unit length (≈ 150 ps/inch for FR4 microstrip, ≈ 180 ps/inch for stripline)

Example — 2 ns rise time (FR4 microstrip)

Lcrit = 2 ns / (6 × 150 ps/in) = 2.2 inch (56 mm)

Any trace longer than ~56 mm with a 2 ns edge must have controlled impedance and termination — regardless of whether the clock is 1 MHz or 100 MHz.

What happens above Lcrit

  • Incident wave arrives at load before source has finished transitioning
  • Reflection travels back to source, causing ringing
  • Ringing amplitude = Γ × Vswing where Γ is the reflection coefficient
  • Undershoot/overshoot can exceed device absolute maximum ratings
  • Multiple reflections cause glitches that false-trigger sequential logic

03

Reflections and Ringing

Impedance mismatches create voltage spikes proportional to tr — not fclk

Reflection coefficient

ΓL = (ZL − Z0) / (ZL + Z0)

ZL = load impedance, Z0 = trace impedance

Ringing example

Unterminated CMOS input (ZL = ∞, Z0 = 50 Ω):
ΓL = (∞ − 50) / (∞ + 50) = +1.0

Full reflection — voltage doubles at load, then the reflected wave returns to the source (ΓS ≈ −0.5 for a typical CMOS driver), bouncing until damped. This occurs on every edge, independent of clock speed.

Ringing characteristics

  • Overshoot can exceed VCC+0.5V — ESD diode conduction
  • Undershoot below GND−0.5V on the low edge
  • Ring frequency set by TD: fring ≈ 1/(4 × TD)
  • Amplitude decays by ΓS × ΓL per round trip
  • Glitches cause false clocking of downstream flip-flops

Fixes

  • Series termination: Rs = Z0 − Zdriver, placed near source
  • Parallel termination at load (AC, Thevenin, or diode)
  • Reduce edge rate with series RC snubber
  • Match PCB trace impedance to driver output impedance
04

EMI from Fast Edges

Radiated emissions follow edge rate — a 1 MHz clock can fail at 350 MHz

EMI spectral envelope

The radiated emissions spectrum of a digital clock has envelope breakpoints at:

f1 = 1 / (π × τ)    f2 = 1 / (π × tr)

τ = pulse width, tr = rise time

Below f1: spectrum rolls off at −20 dB/decade
Between f1 and f2: flat (−20 dB/dec still)
Above f2: rolls off at −40 dB/decade

Key insight

CISPR 32 Class B measures from 30 MHz to 1 GHz. A 1 MHz clock with 1 ns edges has significant harmonic energy across this entire band — it can fail at frequencies 350× higher than the clock.

Practical failure examples

  • 1 MHz SPI clock, 2 ns rise: harmonics at 3, 5, 7… MHz × 1 MHz up to ~175 MHz
  • 10 MHz CAN clock, 1 ns rise: harmonic #35 at 350 MHz — exactly CISPR measurement band
  • 5 MHz PWM, 3 ns rise: harmonics detectable at 117 MHz — AM broadcast interference
  • Any fast GPIO toggle can cause failures even at low toggle rates

Effective EMI reductions

  • Slowing tr from 1 ns to 5 ns reduces emissions above f2 by 14 dB
  • Spread-spectrum clocking randomises harmonic peaks (±0.5% usually sufficient)
  • Ferrite bead on clock line suppresses high-frequency energy in PCB trace
  • Guard traces with stitching vias at < λ/20 spacing
05

Ground Bounce from di/dt

Switching current × package inductance = voltage noise on every edge

Ground bounce mechanism

Vbounce = Lpkg × (di/dt) = Lpkg × Isw / tr

Lpkg = package pin inductance (typically 2–10 nH), Isw = switching current

Example

CMOS output driving 50 Ω, Vswing = 3.3 V:
Isw = 3.3 / 50 = 66 mA
Lpkg = 5 nH, tr = 2 ns:
Vbounce = 5×10⁻⁹ × 0.066/2×10⁻⁹ = 165 mV

This bounces the ground reference of every other output on the same package — corrupting adjacent signals, regardless of the clock frequency.

Vbounce = Lpkg × di/dt is a first-order approximation using a single lumped pin inductance. Real ground bounce also depends on mutual inductance between adjacent pins/bond wires, the quality of the return path (package and PCB), and the switching sequence of simultaneous outputs — treat this as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a precise prediction.

Consequences

  • Ground bounce appears as noise on all other I/O sharing the same GND pin
  • Causes data corruption in parallel buses and byte-wide GPIO
  • Simultaneous switching output (SSO) noise scales with number of switching outputs
  • Vbounce ∝ 1/tr — slower edge rate directly reduces ground bounce

Mitigation

  • Slow edge rates — most effective single measure
  • Use multiple GND pins (reduces effective Lpkg)
  • Local bypass capacitor < 5 mm from IC VCC/GND pins
  • Avoid simultaneous switching of wide buses
  • Use flip-chip packages (much lower inductance than QFP/SOIC)

Real-World Failures — "It's Only a Slow Clock"

Automotive

CAN Bus — 1 Mbit/s

1 MHz bit rate but a 40 ns rise time — equivalent bandwidth ≈ 8.75 MHz. An unterminated 200 mm stub off the bus can ring near its own quarter-wave resonance (order-of-magnitude estimate assuming ~0.6c cable velocity factor; actual frequency depends on cable dielectric, stub termination, and connector parasitics — always confirm with TDR or simulation rather than this estimate alone). ISO 7637-2 pulse testing can excite this resonance and cause ringing-induced false messages — a safety-critical failure mode.

Industrial

RS-485 — 9600 baud

Only 9.6 kHz data rate, but the CMOS driver has a 3 ns rise time → 117 MHz bandwidth. A 3 m cable without termination rings and corrupts data under IEC 61000-4-4 EFT test. "Slow" baud rate was irrelevant.

Consumer

32.768 kHz RTC Clock

Extremely low frequency — but the CMOS buffer driving it has a 2 ns edge, giving it ~175 MHz of equivalent bandwidth. A 150 mm trace to the crystal runs alongside an audio line; capacitive coupling injects that 175 MHz energy directly into the audio path. In the receiver, this doesn't "become" audible on its own — it beats against the radio's local oscillator or another strong RF source in the same non-linear IF stage (intermodulation/mixing), and the difference frequency (here, ≈175 kHz) falls inside the IF passband, where it demodulates into an audible tone. The fix is to keep the coupling from happening at all (shielding, separation, filtering at the RTC buffer), not to explain away the mixing product.

Medical

5 MHz SPI to ADC

5 MHz SCLK seems "medium speed," but the FPGA GPIO has a 0.5 ns edge → ~700 MHz equivalent bandwidth. For an unterminated 80 mm stub (FR4 microstrip, TPD ≈ 150 ps/in ≈ 5.9 ps/mm), one-way delay TD ≈ 80 mm × 5.9 ps/mm ≈ 472 ps; using fring ≈ 1/(4 × TD) from Concept Card 03 gives a ringing resonance around ~530 MHz — well inside the 700 MHz signal bandwidth, so the edge excites it strongly. Under IEC 60601-1-2 immunity test, this ringing can corrupt ADC reads — a patient-safety risk that has nothing to do with the "slow" 5 MHz clock rate.

Common Mistakes

The Six Most Frequent "Slow Clock" SI Errors

M1

Checking clock frequency instead of rise time

Symptom: Design passes at bench, fails at EMC pre-scan at unexpected frequencies

Engineers look at the schematic, see "10 MHz," and apply no SI rules. The CMOS driver's 2 ns edge isn't on the schematic — it's only in the datasheet.

Fix: Read driver datasheet rise/fall time spec. Use that to calculate BW and L_crit before routing.
M2

Long clock traces without termination

Symptom: Intermittent logic errors on slow bus that worsen at low temperature

Temperature affects driver output impedance and load capacitance. Ringing amplitude that is borderline at room temperature crosses the logic threshold at −20 °C or +85 °C.

Fix: Series terminate at source (R_s ≈ Z_0 − R_driver). Works for point-to-point nets regardless of frequency.
M3

Using a fast FPGA GPIO to drive a slow bus

Symptom: 100 kHz I²C bus causes EMI failure at 175 MHz

FPGA I/O configured for maximum drive strength has sub-nanosecond edges by default. I²C at 100 kHz with 0.5 ns edges radiates up to 700 MHz.

Fix: Configure FPGA drive strength and slew rate. Use SLOW slew for any net below 50 Mbps. Add series R if needed.
M4

Ignoring connector and via stubs

Symptom: Board works standalone, fails when cable is attached

A 50 mm connector stub resonates at 1 GHz for a 1 ns rise time edge. The connector pin becomes a λ/4 antenna — even if the PCB trace is correctly terminated.

Fix: Minimise stub length at connectors. Use matched connectors with controlled Z. Terminate at the connector entry point.
M5

Adding a ferrite bead "to fix EMI" on a fast clock

Symptom: Ferrite bead causes more ringing than without it

A real ferrite bead is not a pure inductor: below its self-resonant frequency it behaves inductively, but above self-resonance its impedance becomes dominated by the lossy, resistive part of the core material — this resistive loss is exactly what makes ferrite beads effective, dissipative EMI suppressors rather than simple reactive filters. The trouble is what happens on the way there: while still acting inductively, the bead's inductance combines with downstream trace/pin capacitance into an LC network that can ring at fres = 1/(2π√LC). For a 100 nH bead (in its inductive region) and 20 pF of trace capacitance, fres ≈ 112 MHz — adding a resonance rather than damping one, if that frequency falls inside the signal's bandwidth.

Fix: Slow the edge rate first (series R near the driver). Use ferrite beads only where there is a resistive load to damp the LC resonance, and check the datasheet's impedance-vs-frequency curve to confirm where the bead becomes resistive rather than assuming it is inductive at your operating frequency.
M6

Blaming a "slow" ADC/SPI interface for data errors instead of checking edge rate

Symptom: Low-speed SPI/I²C link to an ADC reads noisy or corrupted data, but the bus clock is only a few MHz

This is one of the most common industry cases of the myth in practice: a SPI clock in the low single-digit MHz range "should" be electrically trivial, so engineers look elsewhere for the bug (grounding, power supply noise, ADC configuration) before checking the driver's actual edge rate. A fast MCU or FPGA GPIO on that same "slow" bus commonly has sub-nanosecond rise/fall times, producing hundreds of MHz of equivalent bandwidth and ringing on any unterminated stub — indistinguishable from "noisy" ADC data on a scope capture unless you specifically look at the edges.

Fix: Before investigating power, grounding, or ADC settings, check the driver datasheet's rise/fall time and probe the actual edge with a sufficiently fast scope. Reduce GPIO drive strength/slew rate or add series termination if the edge rate is driving reflections on the SPI lines.

⚠ Controlled impedance + series termination required.

With < 2 ns rise time, L_crit is under 10 mm. This trace MUST be routed as a controlled-impedance line and terminated with a series resistor (R_s ≈ Z_0 − R_driver) placed within 5 mm of the driver pin. Review for EMI — bandwidth exceeds 175 MHz.

✓ Short enough — but watch EMI and ground bounce.

Trace length is below L_crit. No termination required, but: (1) verify edge rate won't cause ground bounce on adjacent signals; (2) the fast edge still radiates — review PCB layer stackup and shielding if EMC compliance required.

⚠ Termination required.

Trace exceeds L_crit. Add a series termination resistor at the source (R_s = Z_0 − R_driver, typically 22–47 Ω) within 10 mm of the driver. Route as 50 Ω microstrip or stripline. Verify with SPICE or HyperLynx simulation before committing to layout.

✓ Add series resistor — low cost fix.

A 22–33 Ω series resistor at the driver absorbs the reflection without the cost of a parallel termination supply. Capacitive loads improve with series R because the RC low-pass also reduces edge rate, decreasing EMI and ground bounce.

✓ Within limits — verify with simulation for critical signals.

Trace is short enough that reflections damp before the next edge. No formal termination required. Still recommended: add a 0 Ω resistor pad in series so termination can be added during debug without board respins.

⊘ Low risk — no action required.

Slow edges with no EMC requirement and short traces: SI issues are unlikely to cause problems. Consider adding a 0 Ω placeholder for future flexibility. Document the rise time assumption in the design file in case requirements change.

  • Signal: SPI clock at 5 MHz
  • PCB: FR4 microstrip (TPD = 150 ps/inch)
  • Trace length: 85 mm (3.35 inch)
  • Package inductance: 3 nH per pin
  • Load current: 30 mA per output

Calculate:

  1. Signal bandwidth
  2. Critical trace length
  3. Is termination required?
  4. Ground bounce voltage

Solution

1. Signal bandwidth:
BW = 0.35 / t_r = 0.35 / 0.8×10⁻⁹
BW = 437.5 MHz — despite a 5 MHz SPI clock
2. Critical trace length:
L_crit = t_r / (6 × T_PD) = 0.8 ns / (6 × 150 ps/in)
L_crit = 0.8 / 0.9 = 0.89 inch = 22.5 mm
3. Termination required?
Trace = 85 mm, L_crit = 22.5 mm
85 / 22.5 = 3.8× over L_crit
YES — termination is mandatory. Use 33 Ω series R at driver pin.
4. Ground bounce:
V_bounce = L_pkg × I_sw / t_r
V_bounce = 3×10⁻⁹ × 30×10⁻³ / 0.8×10⁻⁹
V_bounce = 112 mV per switching output
Worst-case SSO with 4 outputs: 4 × 112 mV = 450 mV — exceeds noise margin for 3.3 V logic (typ. 300 mV margin).
This "slow" 5 MHz SPI interface needs: controlled-impedance routing, series termination, and reduced FPGA drive strength (switch to SLOW slew) to pass EMC and maintain logic margins. The clock frequency is irrelevant — the 0.8 ns edge is what matters.

Related Concepts

Ten Ideas Worth Understanding Next

This article focuses on why edge rate — not clock frequency — drives SI and EMI behaviour. These related concepts build directly on that idea; each is worth its own deep dive, but a working summary is given here so you know what to look up next.

1. Why digital signals contain harmonics

Any periodic non-sinusoidal waveform can be decomposed into a fundamental frequency plus an infinite series of harmonics. A digital clock is periodic and trapezoidal, so it always radiates and couples energy well above its fundamental frequency — the basis for everything else on this page.

2. Fourier series explanation

A Fourier series expresses a periodic waveform as a sum of sine/cosine terms at integer multiples of the fundamental. For a trapezoidal wave, the harmonic coefficients fall off as sin(x)/x, shaped by pulse width and rise time — this is the rigorous basis for the −20/−40 dB/decade envelope used throughout this article.

3. Why square waves need high-frequency content

A perfectly sharp edge requires infinite bandwidth to reconstruct (Gibbs phenomenon); a real, finite-rise-time edge only needs enough harmonics to reproduce that finite slope. Faster edges therefore always require more high-frequency content than slower edges, regardless of repetition rate.

4. Eye diagram degradation from reflections

An eye diagram overlays many bit periods on top of each other. Reflections from impedance mismatches add timing jitter and amplitude noise that close the eye vertically and horizontally — a direct, measurable consequence of the ringing described in Concept Card 03.

5. Driver output impedance effects

The source reflection coefficient ΓS depends on how well the driver's output impedance matches the trace. Low-impedance CMOS drivers into a 50 Ω trace create significant ΓS, which is exactly what series termination is designed to cancel out.

6. Transmission-line delay vs. rise time

The core comparison behind Concept Card 02: a trace's one-way propagation delay TD versus the signal's rise time tr. When TD is a small fraction of tr, lumped-circuit approximations hold; once it isn't, distributed transmission-line analysis is required.

7. Propagation delay vs. clock period

These are easy to conflate but unrelated in magnitude: propagation delay is a per-trace, per-geometry number (picoseconds to low nanoseconds), while clock period is a system timing budget (often much larger). A trace can be "electrically long" for SI purposes while still being timing-closed for the clock period.

8. Distributed vs. lumped circuits

A lumped model assumes voltage is the same everywhere on a conductor at a given instant; a distributed model accounts for finite propagation speed, so voltage varies with both position and time. The TD > tr/6 threshold is the practical dividing line between the two for digital design.

9. Why edge-rate control reduces EMI

Because the spectral envelope's corner frequencies are set by tr, slowing the edge (series R, controlled slew rate, RC filtering) pushes the −40 dB/decade rolloff to a lower frequency — directly lowering radiated and conducted emissions without touching the clock frequency at all.

10. SI vs. PI relationship

Signal integrity (SI) concerns the quality of a signal on its own net; power integrity (PI) concerns the stability of the supply rails feeding the drivers. They interact directly through ground bounce (Concept Card 05): a fast edge is simultaneously an SI event on its own trace and a PI event on the shared power/ground system.
