Overview

Pre-Compliance vs Accredited Certification Testing

Pre-compliance (or pre-scan) testing is a valuable engineering tool. It lets you identify emission peaks, validate fixes, and estimate certification risk early — before spending thousands on lab time. Done correctly, it dramatically increases first-time pass rates.

What it cannot do is guarantee certification. Accredited testing under ISO/IEC 17025 involves calibrated equipment with traceable uncertainty budgets, validated test environments, and accredited operators following normative test procedures. Each of these differs from a typical engineering bench in ways that shift measured emissions by up to ±6 dB.

Industry statistic: Approximately 50–60% of products fail EMC certification on their first lab visit, even when pre-compliance testing showed passing results. The most common reason is insufficient margin against combined measurement uncertainty.
Engineering rule: Target a minimum 6 dB margin below the limit during pre-compliance. For products with aggressive timelines or high sample variation, target 10 dB.

01

Measurement Uncertainty Budget

Every measurement has error — accredited labs declare it; pre-compliance benches don't

What measurement uncertainty means

Every measurement has contributing errors — receiver accuracy, antenna factor calibration, cable loss, site imperfections, ambient noise. Combined measurement uncertainty (MU) represents the interval around a measured value in which the true value lies with a stated confidence level.

Combined uncertainty formula

U = k × √(u₁² + u₂² + u₃² + ··· + uₙ²)

k = coverage factor (k=2 for ~95% confidence), ui = standard uncertainty of each source

Typical MU budget — radiated emissions at 1 GHz

Source±u (dB)
Receiver calibration1.0
Antenna factor calibration2.0
Cable attenuation0.5
Site attenuation (SAC vs ideal)2.5
Ambient noise0.5
Combined U (k=2)≈ 6.8 dB

What this means in practice

If a product measures 4 dB below the limit at a pre-compliance bench, the true value (in an accredited lab) could be anywhere from 4 + 6.8 = +2.8 dB above the limit to 4 − 6.8 = 10.8 dB below it.

The product could fail even though pre-compliance said it passed.

Accredited lab practice

  • Labs declare MU in their test reports (ISO 17025 requirement)
  • CISPR 16-4-2 defines how to estimate MU for emission measurements
  • ILAC-G8 states the decision rule for pass/fail with declared MU
  • Some labs apply MU to the limit (guard-band approach) — making limits effectively tighter

Pre-compliance approach

  • No formal MU budget is required or typically calculated
  • Uncalibrated antennas add ±3–5 dB of unknown error
  • Consumer-grade spectrum analysers add another ±2–3 dB
  • Total uncharacterised error routinely exceeds ±8 dB
02

Test Environment Differences

OATS, SAC, GTEM, and reverb chambers each produce different numbers

Environment comparison

OATS Open Area Test Site

Gold standard for most CISPR tests. Large ground plane, no reflections, 10 m antenna distance. Ambient noise risk from nearby sources.

SAC Semi-Anechoic Chamber

Most common accredited lab type. Absorber on 5 walls, conductive floor. Correlation to OATS is within ±3 dB per CISPR 16-1-4.

GTEM Cell Gigahertz TEM cell

Compact, usable up to ~18 GHz. Correlation method required per CISPR 16-1-3. Divergence from OATS can be ±6 dB without correction.

Lab room Pre-compliance typical

Metallic walls reflect signals. No absorption. Results can vary by ±15 dB depending on room resonances, furniture, and position.

Room resonance effect

A typical lab room (e.g. 5 × 4 × 3 m) has its lowest room resonance at:

fres = c / (2 × L) = 3×10⁸ / (2 × 5) = 30 MHz

Mode density increases with frequency — most pre-compliance frequency bands are heavily affected

Practical consequences

  • Emission peak at 150 MHz in a 1 m × 1 m room corner can read 12 dB high due to constructive reflection
  • Same peak in the room centre may read 8 dB low
  • Result depends on furniture, cables, and operator position — none of which are controlled
  • Ambient noise from computers, chargers, and monitors corrupts readings below 200 MHz

Minimising the gap

  • Use a GTEM cell with CISPR correlation (more reproducible than open room)
  • Rent accredited chamber time for validation scans before full certification
  • Perform pre-compliance outdoors on a ground plane for OATS correlation
03

LISN Accuracy and Conducted Emissions

A non-standard LISN shifts conducted emission readings by up to 6 dB

What a LISN does

A Line Impedance Stabilisation Network (LISN) — also called an AMN (Artificial Mains Network) — presents a standardised 50 Ω impedance to the EUT at RF frequencies while blocking incoming mains noise. CISPR 16-1-2 defines the impedance vs frequency curve that must be met within ±20% (approximately ±1.5 dB).

Impedance specification

ZLISN(f) = 50 Ω ∥ j2πf·50µH ∥ 1/(j2πf·0.1µF)

Transitions from ~50 Ω at 150 kHz to 50 Ω above 1 MHz (the voltage divider across 50 Ω output)

LISN errors and their impact

LISN issueError
DIY ferrite + resistor LISN±4–6 dB
Consumer LISN, uncalibrated±2–3 dB
Commercial LISN, calibrated±0.5–1 dB
Certified LISN (lab grade)±0.2 dB

Ground plane and bonding

Conducted emission tests require the LISN bonded to a ground reference plane, 800 × 800 mm minimum (CISPR 16). Pre-compliance setups often lack a proper ground reference, adding another ±3 dB variation.

Practical failure scenario

  • Product measures −4 dB at 500 kHz conducted during pre-compliance
  • DIY LISN has +3 dB error at that frequency
  • True reading: −4 + 3 = −1 dB — nearly at the limit
  • Lab also applies 50 Hz isolation differently → product fails at +2 dB
  • Result: confident pre-compliance pass, first-time lab failure

Recommendations

  • Use a CISPR 16-1-2 compliant LISN with current calibration certificate
  • Bond LISN to a proper ≥800 × 800 mm ground plane
  • Verify LISN impedance at key frequencies (150 kHz, 500 kHz, 5 MHz, 30 MHz)
04

Sample-to-Sample Variation

Component and PCB tolerances shift emissions across the production population

Sources of sample variation

SourceVariationEMI impact
PCB trace impedance±10%±2 dB
Bypass capacitor value±20% (X5R)±3 dB at resonance
Ferrite bead impedance±25%±4 dB
Crystal / oscillator frequency±50 ppmharmonic shift
Combined (worst case)—±6–8 dB

Statistical argument

Even if the golden sample you test passes with 4 dB margin, the production population may have units that are 6 dB worse — exceeding the limit. Testing only one or two pre-production units does not characterise the distribution.

What accredited standards require

  • Most standards require testing worst-case configuration (max clock speed, max cable length, max load current)
  • FCC Part 15B: up to 5 samples may be required if the initial sample is borderline
  • IEC 61000 product standards often require testing with all accessories and at multiple EUT orientations
  • RE directive and UKCA require representative samples from production

Mitigation

  • Test at least 3 units during pre-compliance and use the worst result
  • Test worst-case configuration: longest cable, max data rate, max brightness
  • Characterise spread of key EMI-sensitive components with your EMS team
  • Use ±1% capacitors for EMI filter components (not ±20% X5R/X7R)
05

Test Configuration Sensitivity

Cable routing and EUT position change radiated emissions by 6–20 dB

Why configuration matters so much

External cables are the dominant antenna in most radiated emission failures. A 1 m USB cable at 200 MHz is approximately λ/1.5 — an efficient radiator. Its routing, bend radius, and proximity to the ground plane all affect how much common-mode current it carries.

Key configuration variables

VariableEmission change
Cable drape height above ground plane±10 dB
EUT orientation (0° vs 90° vs 270°)±15 dB
Cable bundle tied vs spread±6 dB
EUT height above ground (0.8 m spec)±8 dB
Full turntable rotation sweeprequired

Accredited lab procedure

  • Turntable sweeps 0°–360° in defined increments (typically 5° or 10°)
  • Antenna height scan from 1 m to 4 m at 3 m distance (OATS/SAC)
  • Both horizontal and vertical polarisation measured
  • Cable arrangement defined by standard or agreed with lab
  • Support equipment (charger, PC) included in test boundary

Pre-compliance pitfalls

  • Fixed antenna position misses worst-case orientation
  • No turntable — only one angle measured
  • Cables arranged for convenience, not worst-case
  • Support equipment excluded from measurement area
  • Power supply cable looped on bench — different from lab drape spec

Real-World Certification Failures After Pre-Compliance Pass

Consumer Electronics

Wireless earbuds — FCC Part 15B

Pre-scan: 5 dB margin at 384 MHz. Certification: +2 dB over limit. Root cause: pre-scan used uncalibrated LPDA; cal. factor was 3 dB off at 384 MHz. Combined with 4 dB margin required for MU, the product was borderline from day 1.

Medical Device

Infusion pump — IEC 60601-1-2

Pre-compliance conducted emissions: 6 dB margin. Lab: −2 dB. Root cause: engineering pre-scan used a DIY ferrite+50Ω LISN with +8 dB error at 300 kHz. Fixed by procuring a certified AMN before second lab visit.

Industrial

Motor drive — EN 55011 Class A

Pre-scan in office building: all emissions within limit. Certification in SAC: +8 dB at 150 MHz. Root cause: office building had a room resonance at 152 MHz (due to ceiling pipes) that actually suppressed the emission reading during pre-scan.

Automotive

EV on-board charger — CISPR 25

3 samples pre-scanned. All passed. Certification sample (from production lot) failed at 2nd harmonic by 3 dB. Root cause: X5R capacitor in the EMI filter measured 18% below nominal at the production temperature — shifting the filter corner 40 kHz.

Common Mistakes

Five Ways Pre-Compliance Gives False Confidence

M1

Treating a 3 dB margin as safe

Symptom: Pre-scan passes by 3 dB, certification fails by 2 dB

3 dB is the most common "feels safe" margin — and the most common failure margin. Combined MU of a good pre-scan setup is typically 4–6 dB. A 3 dB measured margin statistically represents about a 50% pass probability at certification.

Fix: Target ≥ 6 dB margin for standard products, 10 dB for medical/automotive where re-test costs are catastrophic.
M2

Testing only the "golden sample"

Symptom: Engineering prototype passes, production unit fails

The golden sample is often hand-assembled, reworked, or has cherry-picked components. It is unrepresentative of the production population. EMI filters built with ±20% capacitors can vary by 3–4 dB across units.

Fix: Pre-test a minimum of 3 production-representative samples. Use the worst result for margin calculation.
M3

Testing without worst-case operating mode

Symptom: Product passes idle pre-scan, fails at full load during certification

EMI standards require testing at worst-case configuration. For a laptop: maximum CPU load, maximum screen brightness, all wireless radios active, with all accessories connected. Idle testing can read 10+ dB better than worst-case.

Fix: Document the worst-case configuration in the pre-compliance test plan and verify it matches what the lab will test.
M4

Not recording the pre-compliance test configuration

Symptom: Cannot reproduce pre-compliance results; certification setup differs

If the pre-compliance test setup is not documented (cable lengths, positions, firmware version, operating mode), it cannot be reproduced. Minor differences in setup between pre-scan and lab visit can invalidate the comparison entirely.

Fix: Photograph the full setup. Record cable part numbers, lengths, and routings. Document firmware version and operating configuration.
M5

Ignoring conducted emissions pre-compliance

Symptom: Radiated emissions pass pre-compliance; conducted emissions fail at lab

Engineers often focus radiated emissions pre-scans but skip conducted emissions because a proper LISN isn't available. Conducted emissions account for approximately 30% of FCC/CE first-time failures and are easier to fix early than radiated.

Fix: Invest in a CISPR 16-1-2 compliant LISN before PCB design is frozen. Conducted emissions fixes (filter components) are cheap early and expensive late.

How Much Pre-Compliance Margin Do I Need?

What is the consequence of a first-time certification failure?

Is the product family or circuit design new (no prior certification data)?

What is the quality of your pre-compliance setup?

Have you tested ≥ 3 representative production samples?

Do you have calibrated equipment (antenna factor + receiver)?

⚠ Target 10 dB margin minimum.

High-stakes new design: 10 dB accounts for combined pre-compliance uncertainty (±4–6 dB), sample variation (±2–3 dB), and leaves a residual buffer. Commission a lab pre-validation scan (renting accredited chamber time without the full test report) before the full certification booking. Document everything.

⚠ Target 8 dB margin.

Medium uncertainty setup: 8 dB covers typical pre-compliance errors and accounts for sample spread. Test at least 3 samples. Book a lab pre-validation scan if schedule allows — a half-day SAC rental is far cheaper than a failed certification cycle.

✓ Target 6 dB margin — minimum acceptable.

Well-controlled pre-compliance with representative samples: 6 dB is defensible. Ensure your test report records setup photos, equipment serial numbers, and calibration certificate dates. Share this documentation with the lab before your certification appointment.

⊘ Pre-compliance results are indicative only — not margin-based.

Without calibrated equipment, you cannot calculate a meaningful margin. Use pre-compliance for qualitative debugging (find and fix obvious peaks) but do not attempt to predict certification outcome. Budget for at least one certification re-test.

Engineering Calculation Exercise

Given:

  • Pre-compliance measurement: 4 dB below the radiated emission limit at 150 MHz
  • Uncalibrated LPDA antenna (estimated antenna factor error: ±3 dB standard uncertainty)
  • Consumer spectrum analyser (±1.5 dB standard uncertainty)
  • Office room test environment (±4 dB standard uncertainty due to reflections)
  • Cable attenuation uncertainty: ±0.5 dB
  • Sample variation from 2 units tested: worst was 1 dB worse than best
  • Coverage factor k = 2 (95% confidence)

Calculate:

  1. Combined measurement uncertainty (U) at k=2
  2. Is the 4 dB margin sufficient to pass certification?
  3. What minimum margin is needed?

Solution

1. Combined uncertainty:
u₁ = 3.0 dB (antenna), u₂ = 1.5 dB (analyser), u₃ = 4.0 dB (site), u₄ = 0.5 dB (cable), u₅ = 0.5 dB (sample, half-range of 1 dB)

RSS = √(3.0² + 1.5² + 4.0² + 0.5² + 0.5²)
RSS = √(9 + 2.25 + 16 + 0.25 + 0.25) = √27.75 = 5.27 dB

U = k × RSS = 2 × 5.27 = 10.5 dB
2. Is 4 dB margin sufficient?
True value could be as high as: measured + U = −4 dB + 10.5 dB = +6.5 dB above limit
NO — the 4 dB margin is completely insufficient. The product could be failing by 6.5 dB.
3. Minimum margin needed:
Margin ≥ U = 10.5 dB to be confident of passing at 95%
The product currently shows 4 dB margin — a 6.5 dB shortfall.

Actions: (1) Reduce emissions by at least 7 dB; OR (2) replace uncalibrated antenna with a calibrated one (reduces U to ~5.4 dB); AND rent a GTEM cell (reduces site uncertainty to ~1.5 dB, reducing U to ~4.1 dB); THEN 4 dB margin is borderline acceptable.
This exercise shows why pre-compliance with poor equipment and a small margin is almost meaningless as a pass/fail predictor. Calibrated equipment and a controlled environment reduce U by 50–60%, dramatically improving pre-compliance predictive value.
Engineering Standards

Relevant Standards & Accreditation Bodies

Standard / Body Scope Pre-compliance relevance
CISPR 16-1-1 EMC measurement apparatus specification Defines the receiver characteristics that accredited labs must meet — calibrated sweep receivers, not spectrum analysers
CISPR 16-1-2 LISN / AMN specification Defines the impedance vs frequency curve a LISN must meet. Non-compliant LISNs invalidate conducted emission results
CISPR 16-1-4 Antennas and test sites Normalised Site Attenuation (NSA) validation defines when a SAC is equivalent to OATS. Not applicable to office rooms
CISPR 16-4-2 Measurement uncertainty for EMC tests The definitive guide for estimating MU in radiated and conducted emission measurements. Used by all accredited labs
ISO/IEC 17025 Accreditation standard for test laboratories Requires labs to declare MU, maintain calibration records, and use validated test methods. Pre-compliance benches are not compliant
ILAC-G8 Decision rules for conformity with specifications Defines the "shared risk" and "guard-band" approaches to making pass/fail decisions when a result is within the MU interval
ANSI C63.4 US measurement methods for radiated emissions Specifies OATS or SAC with NSA validation ≤4 dB. GTEM correlation method also permitted with correction factors
