MODULE 08

Heatsink Material Selection

Compare thermal conductivity, density, and cost across aluminum, copper, graphite, and vapor chamber technologies, and learn the trade-off logic engineers use to pick the right material for a given power density, weight budget, and cost target.

Learning Objectives

  • ✓ Compare the thermal, mechanical, and cost properties of common heatsink materials
  • ✓ Understand why conductivity alone does not determine the best material
  • ✓ Know when copper, graphite, or vapor chambers are justified over aluminum
  • ✓ Use an interactive weighting tool to rank materials against real design constraints
  • ✓ Avoid common material-selection and galvanic-corrosion mistakes

The heat sink material directly determines how efficiently heat spreads from the source to the fin surfaces. However, thermal conductivity alone does not tell the whole story — engineers must balance conductivity against weight, cost, manufacturability, and corrosion resistance for the specific application.

A common engineering mistake is defaulting to copper for every high-power application. Copper has roughly 2.3× the conductivity of aluminum 6061 — but also about 3.3× the density and 3.5× the raw material cost. For many designs, aluminum with better fin geometry outperforms copper on a cost-per-watt-dissipated and weight-per-watt-dissipated basis.

Thermal conductivity (W/m·K) — higher is better for spreading heat.

Copper is the preferred choice when one or more of the following conditions exist:

  • High heat flux density: when the heat source is small (<20 mm × 20 mm) and power density exceeds ~30 W/cm², copper's superior spreading resistance makes a measurable difference (typically 5–8°C lower hotspot temperature).
  • Space-constrained designs: when the heatsink footprint cannot be increased, copper extracts more heat per unit area for the same thickness.
  • Embedded heat pipes: copper bases solder directly to copper heat pipes without galvanic corrosion concerns.
  • Hybrid designs: a copper base plate for spreading, combined with lightweight aluminum fins for convection, gives much of copper's benefit at a fraction of the weight and cost penalty.
Never place bare copper directly against bare aluminum in a humid environment without a protective barrier. The galvanic potential difference (0.6–0.8 V) accelerates corrosion at the joint. Use nickel plating, an anodized interface, or a thermal interface pad as a barrier layer.
Graphite: anisotropic pyrolytic graphite (APG) offers exceptional in-plane conductivity (up to 1500 W/m·K) but poor through-plane conductivity (~5 W/m·K). It excels at spreading heat laterally in thin form factors — smartphones, tablets, thin laptops — typically as 25–100 µm spreader sheets bonded to a metal fin structure, not as a standalone heatsink.

Vapor chambers: flat, sealed copper enclosures containing a wick structure and a working fluid. The very high effective conductivity comes from the latent-heat phase-change cycle inside the chamber, not from bulk material conduction. They excel at converting a concentrated hotspot into a uniform base temperature. Minimum practical size (~50×50 mm) and cost restrict them to high-end applications: gaming laptops, server CPUs, and 5G base stations.

Recommendation

  • Choosing copper purely because it "sounds better": without checking whether the application is flux-limited or spreading-limited, copper often adds cost and weight with no thermal benefit.
  • Ignoring finished-part cost drivers: comparing only raw material cost while ignoring machining time, extrusion tooling, or vapor-chamber assembly labor gives a misleading total cost picture.
  • Mixing dissimilar metals without a barrier: bare copper-to-aluminum joints in humid or outdoor environments corrode rapidly without plating or an isolating TIM layer.
  • Treating graphite as a drop-in heatsink material: its through-plane conductivity is very low, so it must be paired with a metal structure that handles convection to ambient.
