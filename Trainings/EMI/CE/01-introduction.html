Introduction & What Is Conducted Emission

Every switching converter chops current at megahertz rates. Some of that switching energy always leaks onto the wires it's connected to — the AC mains cord, a DC input harness, or a signal cable. That leakage is a conducted emission, and it's measured directly on the cable, long before it ever has a chance to radiate through the air.

Conducted emissions are unwanted electrical noise generated by electronic equipment that propagates along conductive paths such as AC mains, DC input cables, and signal cables. Unlike radiated emissions, the energy stays confined to the conductor itself until it reaches measurement equipment or couples into another nearby system.

Almost every real CE failure starts with the same chain of events: a switching device inside the product creates a fast current or voltage transition, that transition couples onto a nearby cable, and the cable carries the noise straight out of the enclosure and into the AC mains — where a regulator is legally required to measure it.

Animated: How a Conducted Emission Reaches the Test Equipment

Watch the noise packet travel from the switching device, onto the power cable, through the LISN, and into the EMI receiver — where it finally becomes a point on the compliance graph.

Regulators test conducted emissions first, and often at a lower cost than radiated tests, because a huge share of real-world EMC failures are conducted in origin — fixing them here also tends to reduce radiated emissions later, since the same cable driven by common-mode noise is usually the product's dominant unintentional antenna.
