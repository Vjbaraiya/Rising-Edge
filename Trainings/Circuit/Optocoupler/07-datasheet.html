MODULE 07

Reading the Datasheet

A line-by-line dissection of real optocoupler datasheets: absolute maximum ratings and what actually happens beyond them, electrical characteristic tables and test conditions, CTR and derating curves, safety certification tables, and package drawings with creepage callouts.

Learning Objectives

After completing this module, you will be able to:

Navigate a full optocoupler datasheet with confidence
Extract worst-case values (not typicals) for design
Read CTR vs IF, temperature, and aging curves correctly
Verify safety certification tables against project requirements
Check package drawings for creepage and clearance data
Where this fits. Module 6 defined each parameter. This module teaches you to find them, read their test conditions, and pull the right number out of a real sheet, including the curves and the safety table. To avoid reproducing any vendor’s copyrighted document, the interactive sheet below is an original, generic layout for a fictional part (“RX-817”) with values typical of PC817-class parts. The skills transfer directly to real datasheets.
Illustrations and animations are original teaching drawings, not to scale. Values marked representative are typical of common parts; always confirm against the current datasheet or standard you design to.

Datasheets are dense but predictable. Once you know the map, you can find any number in seconds and, more importantly, you can find the conditions attached to it.

Each highlighted item on the sheet explains what the number really means, the conditions attached to it, and the design trap hiding behind it.

“Absolute maximum” means exactly that: the stress beyond which the manufacturer no longer guarantees the part will survive. Nothing in the electrical-characteristics table is guaranteed there, and life shortens as you approach them. They are not design targets.

RatingWhat physically fails beyond it
IF, IFMLED junction overheating and current crowding; accelerated light-output degradation; bond-wire fusing at high pulses
VRLED reverse breakdown: localised heating damages the junction permanently
VCEOCollector–emitter avalanche breakdown; with energy (inductive loads), destruction

Some datasheets (always for logic and gate-driver parts) include a recommended operating conditions table: the range over which the electrical characteristics apply. Typical entries: supply voltage (e.g. 4.5–5.5 V), IF(on) (e.g. 6.5–15 mA for a 6N137-class part), IF(off) or VF(off) (the input must be truly off, e.g. ≤ 250 µA or ≤ 0.8 V), and ambient temperature.

Pro tip — the off-state is specified too. Leakage from a driver pin can partially turn an LED on. A logic coupler with IF(off) ≤ 250 µA (typical figure) can glitch if the driving GPIO leaks, or if the LED has a pull-up through a long cable. Hold the input firmly off.

The electrical characteristics table has three value columns and one conditions column. The golden rule: design with the min or max that hurts you, at conditions corrected to yours. The typical column describes the median part. Roughly half of your production will be worse.

Switching times are only meaningful together with their test circuit. Phototransistor datasheets typically measure with RL = 100 Ω at IC = 2 mA in the active region. That is great for a fast number and unlike almost any real logic interface.

Worked example — translating tr
Datasheet: tr = 4 µs typ, 18 µs max at RL = 100 Ω. Your pull-up: 10 kΩ.
  1. The response-vs-RL curve (typical) shows roughly 60–100 µs at 10 kΩ.
  2. Scale by max/typ (18/4 ≈ 4.5) for a pessimistic bound: several hundred µs is possible for a worst-case part.
  3. For a 1 kHz signal (500 µs half-period) that is marginal, so reduce RL or choose a faster family (Module 5).

Curves are typical unless labelled otherwise. They tell you the shape of a dependency, which you apply as a ratio to a guaranteed number. Four curves matter most: CTR vs IF (often normalised to 1 at 5 mA), CTR vs TA, output current vs VCE, and response time vs RL. Some vendors also publish long-term CTR degradation curves; Module 9 uses them.

Reading log axes correctly

Most current axes are logarithmic. Each decade (0.1–1–10 mA) has the same width, and the minor gridlines for 2, 3 … 9 get closer together towards the next decade. A point one-third of the way through a decade is ≈ 2, not 3.3. Interpolate logarithmically: x = 10(log x1 + f·(log x2 − log x1)).

Design trap — multiplying typical curves as if they were guarantees. The ratio from a typical curve is an estimate. For safety-critical margins, verify on parts from several lots, or specify a guaranteed CTR at your operating point with the vendor.

Derating graphs show the maximum allowed IF, collector power and total power vs ambient temperature. They are usually flat to 25 °C (sometimes higher) and then fall linearly to zero at the maximum temperature. Read them at your worst-case ambient inside the enclosure, next to whatever heats the board, not at the room temperature on the specification sheet.

Worked example
Ptot 200 mW at 25 °C, zero at 110 °C (fictional RX-817). Enclosure ambient 80 °C.
  1. Slope = 200 / (110 − 25) ≈ 2.35 mW/°C.
  2. Allowed at 80 °C: 200 − 2.35 × 55 ≈ 71 mW, just over a third of the headline figure.

Package drawings give dimensions with tolerances, the recommended land pattern and sometimes explicit creepage/clearance callouts. Use them to make sure your footprint does not quietly reduce the certified distances, and to choose between lead-form options.

Design trap — extending pads under the body. Enlarging SMD pads for hand-soldering or “better fillets” moves copper towards the other side and can cut board creepage below the certified package value. Keep the vendor’s land pattern or check the gap explicitly (Module 11).

The ordering section turns one datasheet into dozens of real part numbers: CTR rank letters, lead-form options (DIP, gull-wing SMD, wide-lead), tape-and-reel, and certification options (e.g. a suffix for VDE-tested parts). Each option can change guaranteed values.

Know the map

Features → abs max → electrical → switching → curves → insulation → package → ordering.

Abs max is not a design point

Stay in the recommended range; derate absolute limits with temperature.

Min/max, with conditions

Use the limiting column and translate its test conditions to yours.

Test circuits differ

Convert tr/tf to your RL with the response curve.

Curves are typical ratios

Read shapes on log axes carefully; apply them to guaranteed numbers.

The insulation table certifies

VIORM, creepage, DTI, CTI, PD test: compare with the end-product standard.

The drawing protects the gap

Check land patterns do not erode certified distances.

The ordering code is the product

Rank, lead form and certification option must be in the BOM.

Part D — Engineering Scenarios

Scenario 1 — The “typical” design that failed in production
An input was designed with CTR = 300 % (the value read off the typical CTR curve at 5 mA). In production, 8 % of boards fail the functional test. The datasheet lists CTR 50–600 %. What went wrong?
Analysis. The curve is typical, and the only guarantee is the 50 % minimum (unranked). About half the parts fall below the median and the tail reaches 50 %. Fix: design with CTRmin of the rank you buy, corrected for IF, temperature and ageing (Module 6). Order a ranked part number and increase IF or reduce the required IC as needed.
Scenario 2 — Wide-lead option or not?
A 230 V product for industrial cabinets (PD3, material group IIIa, reinforced) uses the fictional RX-817 in standard 7.62 mm DIP (7.0 mm creepage). The insulation table lists 8.0 mm for option W. Which do you order?
Analysis. At 250 V, PD3, group III, representative basic creepage is 4.0 mm, so reinforced needs 8.0 mm. The standard DIP (7.0 mm) fails; option W (8.0 mm) just meets it. Fix: order option W, confirm that the VDE certificate covers option W, and keep the PCB creepage at ≥ 8 mm too (slot if necessary).
Scenario 3 — Is 18 µs fast enough?
A designer reads tr = 4 µs typ, 18 µs max and concludes a PC817 with a 22 kΩ pull-up can pass a 10 kHz signal (50 µs half-period). Check the reasoning.
Analysis. The 18 µs max applies at RL = 100 Ω in the active region. With 22 kΩ the Miller term dominates: typical rise time is of the order of 100+ µs (response-vs-RL curve), longer than the half-period. Fix: lower RL to ≈ 1 kΩ with enough IF to saturate at end of life, or use a 6N136-class part.
