MODULE 02 — WHY ISOLATION

Why Optocouplers are Used

Module 1 showed what an optocoupler is. This module answers why you would put one in a design — and makes the answer concrete. A 3.3 V microcontroller wired directly to a 400 V motor controller is one fault away from destruction, one ground offset away from data corruption, and one surge away from hurting somebody. We work through safety, ground loops, noise immunity, fault protection, mixed-voltage interfacing, and the regulatory framework — with simulators, calculators, nine worked design examples, and a decision tree that tells you when an optocoupler is (and is not) the right tool.

Consider the most tempting shortcut in industrial electronics: a 3.3 V microcontroller connected directly to the control input of a 400 V motor drive. It works on the bench. Now ask the questions a design review would ask:

What happens during a fault?

An IGBT shorts, a winding flashes over, or a wiring error lands 400 V (or a rectified 565 V DC bus) on the shared signal line. Every component between the drive and the MCU becomes a fuse — including the MCU, its power rail, and everything else on that rail.

What if the industrial ground rises 100 V?

Motor return currents and ground faults routinely lift a machine's local earth tens of volts above the control room's. With a direct connection, that difference drives current through your signal wiring and shifts every logic threshold — data corruption first, burned traces second.

What about a lightning strike?

An indirect strike hundreds of meters away induces kilovolt transients on long cable runs and raises ground potential across the site. A direct electrical path delivers that energy straight into the lowest-impedance (and most expensive) silicon it can find.

Why is direct connection dangerous?

Because a conductor cannot distinguish signal from fault. Whatever voltage, noise, or surge exists on one side is offered to the other, at the full available fault energy, forever.

Shock hazards in numbers

Isolation requirements exist because the human body is a resistor of roughly 1–2 kΩ (hand-to-hand, wet skin much lower) connected to ground. Safety standards treat voltages above roughly 42.4 V peak AC or 60 V DC as hazardous to touch. What current does to a person:

Current through body (50/60 Hz) Physiological effect
0.5–2 mA Perception threshold, tingling
5–10 mA Painful shock; muscle control affected
10–20 mA "Let-go" threshold exceeded — cannot release the conductor
30–50 mA Respiratory difficulty; possible asphyxia
50–100 mA+ Ventricular fibrillation risk — potentially lethal
≥ 10 μA (patient-connected) Medical limit: current applied directly to the heart can fibrillate at microamp levels

Galvanic isolation protects on every axis at once: user protection (no conductive path from hazardous voltage to touchable parts), operator and patient safety (leakage currents limited to microamps), equipment protection (faults cannot propagate into the control side), functional safety (E-stop and interlock signals remain trustworthy in fault conditions), and fire prevention (fault energy cannot dump into wiring never sized for it). It is required wherever high-voltage supplies, industrial machinery, grid-connected equipment, laboratory instruments, or medical devices meet human beings.

Medical equipment isolation

Medical devices are the most demanding isolation environment because the "user" may be an anesthetized patient with electrodes bypassing the skin's protective resistance — ECG front ends, patient monitors, defibrillators, infusion pumps, and ultrasound probes all make deliberate electrical contact. IEC 60601-1 therefore limits patient leakage current to microamps and defines protection in terms of MOPP (Means of Patient Protection) and MOOP (Means of Operator Protection). Each "means" is one independent barrier; a mains-to-patient path needs two MOPP — which a single reinforced-insulation optocoupler (VDE 0884-17 certified, 8 mm creepage) can provide as 2×MOPP in one component.

Industrial automation safety

In a factory cabinet, isolation is the containment strategy: PLC I/O racks isolate every field signal so that a ground fault on one sensor loop, a welded contactor, or a miswired 230 V line onto a 24 V terminal damages one input channel — not the backplane, not the CPU, and not the emergency-shutdown chain. Motor drives isolate gate drivers and feedback so a power-stage explosion (they happen) leaves the controller alive to report it. Robotics and safety systems isolate E-stop loops so the stop command survives the very faults it exists to handle.

Power supply isolation

Every offline AC/DC supply — flyback, forward, or LLC — has a transformer carrying power across the mains barrier. But regulation needs information to flow backwards: the secondary-side output voltage must steer the primary-side PWM controller. That return path is the classic optocoupler application. A TL431 shunt regulator on the secondary compares the output against its reference and drives the optocoupler LED; the phototransistor pulls the controller's feedback pin on the primary. Power crosses in the transformer; the error signal crosses as light.

Animated Flyback Feedback Loop

Why ground loops occur

"Ground" is a convenient fiction. Two earth connections 100 m apart are joined by soil, structural steel, and cable shields — all of which carry return currents from motors, welders, and rectifiers. The result is a ground loop: connect two grounded devices with a signal cable, and the volts of difference between their grounds drive amps of circulating current through your cable shield and signal return. Causes stack up in industrial environments: ground reference differences, long cable runs, multiple grounding points, earth potential differences after faults, and magnetically induced loop EMF from nearby high-current conductors.

The same loop-breaking logic prevents circulating currents wherever ground potentials legitimately differ: battery banks whose negative rails float relative to chassis, motor drives whose "power ground" bounces with every PWM edge, multi-cabinet control systems, and DC fast chargers where the vehicle and the charger have separate earthing. Break the metallic path, and each system keeps its own reference in peace.

Common-mode voltage

Common-mode voltage is the voltage both signal conductors share relative to earth. A motor-drive phase node swings the entire gate-drive circuit hundreds of volts in tens of nanoseconds; a solar string floats hundreds of volts above ground; an RS-485 link between buildings rides on whatever the two earths disagree by. Electronics connected directly across such domains see this voltage as if it were signal. An optocoupler sees it only as stress across its barrier — the detector never knows.

Term Definition Typical spec
CMR (Common-Mode Rejection) How completely a static/low-frequency common-mode voltage is kept out of the output Expressed in dB, or as max VCM
CMTI (Common-Mode Transient Immunity) The fastest common-mode dv/dt the device tolerates without corrupting its output state 15–50 kV/μs (gate drivers 100+ kV/μs)

CMTI matters because of the ~0.5 pF barrier capacitance: a fast common-mode edge drives displacement current \(i = C \cdot dv/dt\) through that capacitance into the detector node. At 50 kV/μs, 0.5 pF passes 25 mA — enough to falsely switch an unprotected output. IGBT, SiC-MOSFET, and GaN gate drivers live on exactly such edges (SiC and GaN reaching 100–200 kV/μs), which is why gate-drive optocouplers advertise CMTI as a headline spec and use internal shields between barrier and detector.

EMI protection

Interference reaches circuits by four couplings: conducted (along shared wiring), capacitive (electric field between adjacent conductors), inductive (magnetic field linking loops), and radiated (far-field pickup on cables acting as antennas). Galvanic isolation attacks the two worst offenders directly: it severs the conducted path entirely, and its sub-picofarad barrier presents ~300 kΩ at 1 MHz to capacitive coupling — versus milliohms for a copper connection. Loop area reduction (Module 11) then handles the magnetic terms. The optical link itself neither emits nor receives electromagnetic interference — photons in silicone are immune to both E and H fields at these energies.

Switching noise: dv/dt, di/dt, ground bounce

PWM systems — buck converters, motor bridges, Class-D stages — generate the noise they must then survive. Every edge produces dv/dt (displacement currents through every stray capacitance), di/dt (inductive kick \(v = L \cdot di/dt\) across every nanohenry of wiring — 10 A/ns through 5 nH is 50 V), and ground bounce as return currents lift the "quiet" reference. High-voltage transients arrive from many directions: ESD (kilovolts from a charged human, sub-nanosecond edges), inductive kickback (any interrupted coil — relay, solenoid, contactor — flies back to whatever voltage it needs), utility switching (capacitor-bank and load switching on the grid), and industrial switching (contactors and drives sharing your feeder). The isolation barrier is the last line that keeps these events on the side where they happened.

Lightning rarely strikes the equipment; it doesn't need to. An indirect strike raises local ground potential by kilovolts and induces surges on every long cable run — the reason outdoor equipment, solar installations, and plant-wide networks specify isolation plus surge protection as a hierarchy: gas discharge tubes and MOVs clamp the bulk energy, series impedance limits current, TVS diodes clamp the remnant, and the galvanic barrier withstands what leaks past (its VIOSM/VIOTM impulse rating — Module 1) without ever offering the surge a path into the protected domain.

Fault containment architecture

System architects use isolation to segment designs into fault domains, so that any single failure is contained where it occurs. The fault tree below reads bottom-up: without barriers, every leaf event propagates to the top; with barriers, propagation stops at the dashed lines.

Fail-safe design. Isolation also simplifies the safety argument: an optocoupler-based E-stop input is dark (off = safe) when unpowered, when the LED fails, when the cable is cut, and when the field supply dies. All credible failures land on the safe side — the property safety assessors call "fail-safe by construction."

Real products are voltage zoos. Logic runs at 3.3 V (or 1.8/5 V), industrial I/O standardized on 24 V decades ago, telecom and server backplanes use 12/48 V, control power arrives at 110 V DC in substations, mains is 230/400 V AC, and EV batteries reach 800 V. These domains must exchange signals constantly — and every boundary crossing is a design decision: level-shift (shared ground, same fault domain) or isolate (independent domains).

Resistor calculations

LED side, targeting \(I_F\) = 5 mA from a 3.3 V GPIO (VF ≈ 1.2 V):

\( R_1 = \dfrac{3.3 - 1.2}{5\,\text{mA}} = 420\,\Omega \rightarrow \textbf{390–430}\,\Omega \) standard value.

Check the GPIO can source 5 mA (most can; use a transistor buffer if not). Output side: PLC sink-type input typically needs > 2 mA at > 11 V to register ON. With CTRmin 130% (B bin) derated ×0.5 EOL: IC = 5 × 1.3 × 0.5 = 3.25 mA — comfortably above threshold, margin 1.6×.

Power check: R1 dissipates I²R = 10.5 mW; LED 6 mW — trivial, any 0402/0603 works.

FPGA to industrial equipment

FPGAs raise the stakes on both sides of the interface: their I/O banks are expensive, low-voltage (1.2–3.3 V), ESD-sensitive, and often routed to fine-pitch BGAs that cannot be field-repaired — while their applications (machine vision triggers, motion-control encoders, industrial Ethernet PHY resets) reach into electrically hostile machinery. The isolation calculus is asymmetrical: a 30-cent optocoupler protecting a $300 FPGA on a $3000 assembly. Fast logic-output couplers (or digital isolators for >10 Mbps links) are standard practice on every FPGA pin that leaves the cabinet; the FPGA's speed advantage is preserved by choosing the coupler family to match the signal rate, not by omitting the barrier.

Standards exist because the market cannot verify safety by inspection: a buyer cannot see whether a barrier withstands 4 kV. Certification converts invisible engineering into legal market access — CE, UL, and CB marks are entry tickets to their respective markets, and the isolation component's certificates are load-bearing documents in every product safety file. The key standards:

IEC 61010-1 — lab & industrial

Measurement, control, and laboratory equipment. Defines measurement categories (CAT I–IV), creepage/clearance by working voltage and pollution degree, and Hi-Pot test levels. Governs multimeters, PLCs, and test instruments.

IEC 60601-1 — medical

Medical electrical equipment. Patient vs operator protection (MOPP/MOOP), leakage current limits in µA, applied-part classes (B/BF/CF), and 2×MOPP reinforced barriers between mains and patient.

UL 1577 — component isolation test

The optocoupler's own recognition standard: defines the VISO withstand rating (e.g. 5 kVrms, 60 s) and 100% production Hi-Pot testing (1 s at 120% of rated). Says nothing about continuous working voltage — that is VDE 0884's job.

VDE 0884-17 / IEC 60747-17 — safe barrier physics

Certifies the barrier for life: rated working voltage VIORM, partial-discharge testing (detects microscopic voids before they become failures), impulse withstand, and the reinforced/basic classification — the certificate that legally supports "reinforced insulation" claims.

IEC 62368-1 — AV & ICT equipment

Hazard-based safety for consumer electronics and networking: classifies energy sources (ES1/ES2/ES3), requires barriers between ES3 (mains) and touchable ES1 circuits, replacing legacy 60950-1/60065.

ISO 26262 / AEC-Q — automotive

Functional safety (ASIL levels) plus AEC-Q102 qualification for optoelectronics: EV traction, chargers, and BMS isolation must carry both the safety rating and automotive stress qualification.

1. Isolating what doesn't need it

Optocouplers on same-board, same-ground signals add cost, delay, and two failure modes for zero benefit. Isolation is for crossing domains, not decoration.

2. Ignoring CTR degradation

The design that "works" at nominal CTR fails at year seven. Stack the margins: bin minimum × temperature × 0.5 end-of-life.

3. Insufficient isolation voltage

Sizing to the working voltage alone, forgetting surges and the required insulation class. The barrier must survive the site's worst day, not its average one.

4. Poor creepage / clearance

A 5 kV part with 2 mm of PCB between its input and output pads is a 2 mm design. Spacing rules apply to the board, not just the component.

5. Ground plane under the barrier

A pour sneaking beneath the package shorts the creepage distance through the board and adds coupling capacitance — keep the barrier zone copper-free on all layers.

6. LED overdrive

Driving 20 mA "for margin" accelerates the very aging that erodes margin. 2–10 mA is the reliability sweet spot.

7. Ignoring standards until layout is done

Insulation class, creepage, and certified components must be chosen at architecture time. Retrofitting 8 mm spacing into a finished board means a respin.

8. Wrong package for the voltage

A 4-pin mini-flat with 5 mm creepage cannot serve a reinforced 230 V barrier needing 8 mm — wide-body exists for a reason.

9. No derating documentation

If the review can't see your worst-case math (CTR, temperature, aging, tolerances), it didn't happen. Module 9 provides the worksheet.

Part E — Engineering Scenarios

Scenario 1 — FPGA meets a noisy 24 V motor controller
"My FPGA communicates with a noisy 24 V motor controller over a 3-wire interface at 1 Mbps, cable length 4 m, separate supplies and grounds." Should an optocoupler be used? Why? What isolation voltage is required, and which package is suitable?
Yes — isolate. Separate grounds + motor PWM noise + 4 m of cable is the textbook ground-loop/common-mode case, and an FPGA bank is the most expensive victim available. At 1 Mbps a phototransistor part is too slow: use high-speed logic-output couplers (6N137/HCPL-2601 class, 10 Mbps) or a digital isolator. Isolation voltage: the working voltage is only 24 V, but surges from the motor environment set the need — 2.5 kVrms VISO (functional/basic) is standard and cheap; an 8-pin DIP or SO-8 package with ≥ 4 mm creepage is ample. Power the field side from the controller's 5 V, keep the FPGA side on its own rail, and route the two grounds to their own connector pins.
Scenario 2 — The sensor that reads garbage every time the crane runs
A 4–20 mA level sensor 80 m away reads perfectly — until the overhead crane operates, when readings jump ±15%. Both the sensor housing and the PLC cabinet are earthed. Diagnose and fix.
Diagnosis: ground loop. Two earth points 80 m apart + crane motor return currents = volts of transient ground difference driving current around the sensor-cable-PLC loop, superimposing error on the 4–20 mA signal. Fix: break the loop — an isolated analog input channel (optocoupler-isolated ADC or isolation amplifier), or an isolated 4–20 mA repeater at the cabinet. Grounding the shield at one end only and twisted-pair cable help, but only isolation removes the galvanic path. Verify by measuring AC voltage between the two earths during crane operation (expect volts).
Scenario 3 — How many MOPP does the pulse oximeter need?
A mains-powered patient monitor connects a fingertip SpO2 sensor (applied part, type BF) to its internal 3.3 V processing board, which is fed from an offline SMPS. Where are the barriers, and what class must each be?
Two barriers. (1) Mains → secondary: the SMPS transformer plus its feedback optocoupler must provide 2×MOPP reinforced insulation (4 kVrms test, 8 mm creepage at 230 V). (2) Processing board → patient-applied part: type BF requires the patient connection to float; the SpO2 interface (LED drive + photodiode signal) crosses a second barrier rated for patient protection with leakage ≤ 100 µA normal condition. A single component failure anywhere must leave one intact means of protection — which is exactly what the MOPP accounting verifies.

Design guidelines

  • Isolate at domain boundaries — where grounds, voltages, or safety classes change — and nowhere else.
  • Choose insulation class first (functional/basic/reinforced), from the end-product standard; it drives component and package selection.
  • Size the barrier for the environment's transients (surge class), not just the working voltage.
  • Stack CTR margins: bin minimum × temperature × 0.5 end-of-life ≥ 2× the required output current.
  • Match coupler family to signal speed; use digital isolators above ~10 Mbps.
  • Preserve the barrier on the PCB: creepage, clearance, no copper under the package (Module 11).
  • Document the worst-case math — reviews and auditors both read it.
