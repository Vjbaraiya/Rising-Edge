MODULE 08

Component Selection Criteria

A systematic selection methodology: derive the isolation requirement from the application, filter by speed and output type, size CTR with lifetime margin, respect temperature and package constraints, and optimize cost across volume scenarios — ending in a reusable selection worksheet.

Learning Objectives

After completing this module, you will be able to:

Derive isolation requirements from system architecture
Filter device families by speed and output needs
Apply CTR margin methodology for lifetime reliability
Trade off package, temperature, and certification constraints
Optimize selections for cost at volume
Where this fits. Modules 5–7 taught you the families, the parameters and the datasheet; Module 9 the ageing margins. This module puts them in order: a repeatable funnel that turns an application into a short list of parts that can be defended in a design review. The isolation numbers here are representative IEC 60664-1-style values; your end-product standard (Module 16) is the authority.
Illustrations and animations are original teaching drawings, not to scale. Values marked representative are typical of common parts; always confirm against the current datasheet or standard you design to.

Selection goes wrong when it starts from a favourite part or from price. The robust order is: hard safety constraints first, then function, then life, then manufacturing, and only then cost. Each stage removes candidates, so you optimise price among parts that already work.

The isolation requirement is derived from the system, not from the part. Five inputs decide it:

Speed decides the family before anything else in the datasheet matters. Use the data rate and the timing accuracy you need:

Worked example — PWM duty accuracy
20 kHz PWM (50 µs period) must keep duty error ≤ 0.5 %.
  1. Allowed PWD = 0.005 × 50 µs = 250 ns.
  2. A phototransistor (µs-class asymmetry) cannot meet it; a 6N137-class part (PWD ≤ ≈ 35 ns) passes easily.

For CTR-specified families, select the rank from the end-of-life requirement, not the new-part behaviour (Modules 6 and 9):

Required CTRmin (rank) = CTRneeded × margin / (kIF · kT · kVCE · kage)    margin ≈ 2 for switching
Worked example — choosing the rank
Circuit needs CTR 25 % at end of life (Module 4 saturation checker). Factors: 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.9 × 0.5 = 0.324. Margin 2.
  1. Required rank minimum = 25 % × 2 / 0.324 ≈ 154 %.
  2. Rank B (130 %) is not enough; rank C (200 %) passes. Alternatively raise IF (costs life) or halve the needed CTR with a larger pull-up (costs speed).
For logic and driver parts Select on the IF threshold instead: design IF ≥ 1.5–2 × the maximum threshold at end of life, and within the recommended IF(on) range.

Match the part’s rated range to the internal ambient of the enclosure, which is often 15–30 °C above the room:

GradeTypical ambient rangeNotes

The selection question is not “is 85 °C inside the rating” but “does the part still meet CTR, leakage and derating at the enclosure’s worst temperature for the full life” (Module 9). A part rated to 110 °C running at 85 °C has far more derating headroom than one rated to 100 °C.

Design trap — certificate options. The certificate lists which ordering options it covers. The wide-lead, SMD and standard versions may have different entries, or none. Match the BOM part number to the certificate.

Only now does price matter. Four cost levers, in order of impact:

Family

A phototransistor is typically an order of magnitude cheaper than a logic coupler or isolator channel; do not buy speed you do not need.

Volume tier

Price per part falls steeply with volume; quote at your real annual quantity.

Channel count

Multi-channel parts and digital isolators amortise cost per channel.

Hidden BOM

Supplies, bypass caps, pull-ups, TVS, board area and test time. An isolated DC-DC can cost more than all the couplers together.

Application B — 230 V flyback feedback (IEC 62368-1, reinforced)

StepDecision
IsolationMains primary to SELV secondary: reinforced; working 250 Vrms, OVC II, PD2 → creepage ≈ 5.0 mm, clearance ≈ 3.0 mm (representative), VIORM ≥ 354 Vpk plus the flyback reflected-voltage peak across the barrier.

Temperature / packageInside the supply up to 100–105 °C; standard DIP (≈ 7 mm) or wide-lead for margin; VDE-certified option.

Application C — isolated RS-485 at 1 Mbit/s in a 400 V drive

StepDecision
IsolationTouchable communication port vs 400 V (up to 300 V line-to-earth), OVC III, PD2 inside the enclosure: reinforced → creepage ≈ 6.3–8 mm, clearance ≈ 5.5 mm (6 kV impulse), VIORM ≥ the DC-bus-referenced peak.
Speed / output1 Mbit/s: PWD ≤ 100 ns and CMTI ≥ 2× drive dV/dt at the port (e.g. ≥ 25–50 kV/µs) → logic coupler or digital isolator.
ChannelsDI, RO and DE: three channels plus isolated 5 V supply.
ResultThree 10–15 MBd logic couplers in stretched SO6 (8 mm) or a 3-channel digital isolator + isolated transceiver; cost comparator usually favours the integrated isolated transceiver at volume (Module 15).

Part D — Engineering Scenarios
Scenario 1 — The cheapest part that failed certification
A team picks a SOP-4 phototransistor (5 mm creepage) for a 230 V product to be sold for installation in industrial cabinets (PD3, reinforced). It is the cheapest option. What should the funnel have caught?
Analysis. Step 1: at 250 V, PD3, group III, representative basic creepage is 4.0 mm, so reinforced needs 8.0 mm. The SOP-4 is out before price is considered. Fix: a stretched SO6 or wide-lead DIP with a VDE certificate covering that option; then compare prices among the survivors.
Scenario 2 — Rank or current?
A circuit needs 30 % CTR at end of life. Factors multiply to 0.36; margin 2. Rank B is 130 % min, rank C 200 % min. Which rank?
Analysis. Required minimum = 30 % × 2 / 0.36 ≈ 167 %. Rank B fails, rank C passes. Raising IF to rescue rank B would shorten LED life (Module 9), so order rank C.
Scenario 3 — Optocoupler or digital isolator at volume?
A 4-channel 1 Mbit/s interface ships 200 000 units/year. The far side has no isolated supply yet. Option 1: four 6N137-class couplers + isolated 5 V DC-DC. Option 2: a 4-channel digital isolator with integrated isolated power. How do you decide?
Analysis. Both need far-side power, which dominates cost. Compare total BOM: four couplers + bypass caps + discrete DC-DC vs one integrated part (plus its caps). Also compare board area, CMTI, PWD and qualification. At high volume and 4 channels the integrated isolator is often cheaper and smaller; the couplers win on simplicity, availability and where analog or fail-safe behaviour matters (Module 15). Document the comparison with real quotes.
